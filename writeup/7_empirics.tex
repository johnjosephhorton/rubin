In this section we empirically test the implications of our model.
The predictions we take to the data are those of the short-run problem of Section~\ref{sec:shortrun}, in which the boundaries of jobs are fixed and AI acts only on how the steps within them are executed.
This is a deliberate restriction rather than a limitation of the theory.
The long-run margins of Section~\ref{sec:longrun} require firms to reorganize and workers to retrain, and not enough time has passed since AI tools became widely available for these adjustments to appear in the data we observe.
What we can observe is where AI is being deployed inside workflows as they are currently arranged, which is precisely what the short-run problem speaks to.
The three predictions we test are: (1) AI-executed steps tend to appear next to each other in the production sequence, forming AI chains; (2) occupations in which AI-friendly steps are more dispersed have fewer of those steps actually executed by AI; and (3) the AI-execution status of a step's neighbors affects the likelihood that the step itself is executed by AI.

For our analysis, we construct a dataset that records three attributes of each step: its AI exposure status (exposed or unexposed to AI), which we interpret as the difficulty of the step for AI, its realized mode of execution (manual, AI-augmented, or AI-automated), and its position in the production sequence.
To create this dataset, we combine data from four sources:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The O*NET 27.3 Database \citep{onet27_3},
    \item Human-generated AI exposure labels for O*NET tasks from \cite{eloundou2023gpts},
    \item Realized AI execution labels for O*NET tasks from the Anthropic Economic Index \citep{handa2025economictasksperformedai},
    \item A GPT-generated task ordering for each O*NET occupation.
\end{enumerate}
The first three sources are existing datasets.
The O*NET database is the standard decomposition of work in the United States: it breaks each of more than 850 occupations into a list of constituent tasks, roughly 18,000 in total, and has served as the backbone of a large empirical literature on the task content of jobs and their exposure to new technologies \citep[e.g.,][]{autor2013putting, deming2017growing, brynjolfsson2018can, webb2020impact, felten2021occupational}.
\cite{eloundou2023gpts} map these O*NET tasks to AI exposure: they develop a rubric asking whether access to an LLM would reduce the time required to perform a task by at least half while preserving output quality, and apply it to every task using both human annotators and GPT-4.
We use their human-generated labels and treat their E1 category as exposed to AI and the remaining categories as unexposed, which yields a conservative measure of exposure.
Whereas these labels capture what AI could in principle do, the Anthropic Economic Index \citep{handa2025economictasksperformedai} captures what AI actually does: it maps millions of anonymized conversations between users and Claude, Anthropic's AI assistant, to O*NET tasks and classifies each conversation by whether the interaction augments or automates the task, providing a task-level measure of realized AI usage.

None of these sources, however, records the order in which an occupation's tasks are performed, and our model's predictions turn precisely on where a task sits in the production sequence.
We therefore construct the workflow orderings ourselves: for each O*NET occupation, we prompt GPT-5-mini \citep[through Expected Parrot;][]{Horton2024EDSL} with the occupation's complete set of tasks and ask it to determine the most reasonable order in which these tasks would be performed in practice.
Generating sequences this way inevitably introduces some noise and arbitrary choices, most notably the wording of the prompt.
We therefore validate the orderings in two ways.
First, we benchmark every result against placebos that randomly reshuffle task positions within occupations; the GPT-generated orderings differ sharply from these placebos, indicating that they capture meaningful workflow structure.
Second, the orderings overlap substantially across ten deliberately varied alternative prompts, and our headline results closely mirror those under the main prompt when re-run on these alternative sequences (Appendix~\ref{app:gptPrompts_robustness}).
The details of how the dataset is constructed are given in Appendix~\ref{app:data_prep}.

The structure of our theoretical framework closely aligns with the structure of the O*NET dataset.
Each production step in the model corresponds to an O*NET task, and each occupation's workflow corresponds to the model's step sequence.
In the absence of AI, a step (that is, an O*NET task) is executed manually and maps directly into a task under our model's definition.
Thus, without AI, an O*NET task can be viewed as both a step and a task, which matches the standard treatment of tasks in the O*NET dataset.
Under AI execution, however, multiple steps, corresponding to a subset of O*NET tasks, may be chained together and executed jointly, forming a composite task as defined in Section~\ref{sec:shortrun}.
In the rest of this section, we use the terms step, O*NET task, and task interchangeably and expect the reader to keep these distinctions in mind.
We similarly use the terms workflow and occupation interchangeably without restating the mapping each time.\footnote{One feature of the data shapes how we use it throughout. The model's automated versus augmented distinction is defined by production sequencing, whereas the Anthropic labels are assigned on task-level feasibility and are agnostic to workflow position, so the data occasionally shows an automated task followed by a manual one, which the model's definition of a chain does not allow. We therefore do not distinguish the two labels in what follows and treat both as indicating AI execution. Appendix~\ref{app:data_prep} documents the discrepancy and provides examples.}


\subsection{Prediction \#1: Tendency to Have Runs of Consecutive AI-executed Tasks}
\label{sec:chainLength_prediction}

The first prediction of the model that we test is that AI steps tend to appear in contiguous blocks rather than being scattered in the workflow.
This prediction follows from the existence of AI chains in our model: if firms deploy AI, chaining can lower costs because the worker verifies the output only once, at the end of the chain, rather than after every step.

To test this, we compute the observed average AI chain length in the data and benchmark it against two randomization placebos.
First, we permute task positions within each occupation's workflow.
Second, we reassign tasks, each carrying its AI-execution label, randomly across occupations within the same major SOC group, preserving each occupation's task count and randomizing task positions within each occupation.\footnote{
We confine the reassignment to major occupation groups rather than the full dataset because tasks remain meaningful within their broad field but not across fields: reassigning a manufacturing task to a software occupation makes little sense, whereas reassigning tasks between two software occupations does.
If anything, restricting the reshuffle to major-group boundaries is the more conservative choice: each major group retains its AI intensity under the within-group reassignment, whereas randomizing tasks across the full dataset would scatter AI-executed tasks over all occupations and push the placebo average chain length even lower.}
The two placebos isolate different margins: the first holds each occupation's task composition fixed and randomizes the ordering of its workflow, while the second holds each occupation's size and each major group's AI intensity fixed and randomizes which tasks, and hence which execution labels, an occupation contains.

Figure~\ref{fig:aiChains_graphs_def1} shows the placebo distribution of average AI chain length across 1{,}000 task position permutations (left) and 1{,}000 task AI-execution label reassignment permutations (right), and compares each with the observed mean AI chain length, marked by the dashed vertical line in each panel.
We observe that the average AI chain length in the data is noticeably larger than in both placebo distributions.
This indicates that AI-executed tasks tend to cluster within occupations, forming longer AI chains than would arise under either type of randomization.
At the same time, the modest magnitude of 1.45 indicates that long AI chains remain rare, perhaps reflecting the early stage of AI adoption; our model predicts chains to lengthen as AI quality improves.

Were the GPT-generated workflow sequences arbitrary, or the Anthropic execution labels distributed randomly across occupations, the observed average AI chain length would be a typical draw from the corresponding placebo distribution.

\subsection{Prediction \#2: Dispersion of AI-able Tasks Affects AI Execution Outcomes}
\label{sec:fragmentation_prediction}

The second prediction of the model that we test is that the extent of realized AI execution in an occupation is determined not just by the AI exposure status of its steps, but also by how clustered or dispersed those AI-able steps are in the workflow.
To examine this, we ask whether, among occupations with similar AI exposure, realized AI execution is lower where AI-able tasks are more dispersed in the production sequence.
Specifically, we estimate the following regression:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \text{ai\_execution}_{o} = {} & \beta_0 + \beta_1 \, \text{ai\_exposure}_{o} + \beta_2 \, \text{empirical\_fragmentation\_index}_{o} \\
    & + \beta_3 \, \text{num\_ai\_able\_steps}_{o} + \varepsilon_o,
\end{aligned}
    \label{eq:fragmentation_index_regression}
\end{equation}
where \emph{$\text{ai\_execution}_{o}$} is the share of tasks executed by AI in occupation $o$, \emph{$\text{ai\_exposure}_{o}$} is the share of AI-exposed tasks, and \emph{$\text{empirical\_fragmentation\_index}_{o}$} measures how dispersed the AI-able tasks are across the occupation's workflow. We additionally control for the number of AI-exposed steps in the occupation. Because the fragmentation index mechanically depends on the number of AI-able steps a workflow contains, this control ensures that $\beta_2$ is identified from how those steps are arranged rather than how many there are.

The model predicts that occupations with higher AI exposure should have a higher share of steps executed by AI ($\beta_1 > 0$).
Conditional on exposure, we should also expect less AI execution where AI-able steps are more dispersed ($\beta_2 < 0$).
The intuition, discussed in Example~\ref{ex:fragmentation} of Subsection~\ref{sec:fragmentation}, is that a chain can span only contiguous steps: clustering AI-able steps creates chaining opportunities that dispersion forecloses, leaving a step that would have been automated inside a chain to be executed on its own or performed manually.

To measure the fragmentation of AI-able tasks in the data, we adapt the fragmentation index of Section~\ref{sec:fragmentation} to our empirical setting.
The \emph{empirical fragmentation index} (EFI) is the ratio of the number of \emph{potential} tasks, as defined by our theoretical model, to the total number of steps, which is fixed and is measured by the raw count of O*NET tasks in that occupation.
The following example clarifies the definition.
Consider an occupation with five O*NET tasks.
If none of the five tasks are exposed to AI, then the number of tasks in the theoretical sense is five, and the EFI equals $5/5 = 1$.
If instead two consecutive tasks are exposed to AI (and thus can form an AI chain of length two when all AI-exposed tasks turn into AI-executed tasks), the number of tasks in the model falls to four and the EFI becomes $4/5 = 0.8$.
Thus, the empirical fragmentation index decreases as more AI-exposed steps can potentially be consolidated into longer AI chains.\footnote{The EFI is the special case of \eqref{eq:fragmentation} in which every step takes one unit of time in either mode, $\manualTime{i} = \AItime{i} = 1$, and AI either always or never succeeds at a step, $q_i \in \{0,1\}$, normalized by the number of steps.
It therefore captures the arrangement of a workflow's AI-able steps, which is what we observe, and not the differences in time cost and reliability that the theoretical index also prices.
Since $\manualTime{i} = 1$ satisfies the hypothesis of \eqref{eq:fragmentation_closed_form}, the EFI is exactly one minus the share of adjacent AI-able pairs in the workflow, so it inherits the ordering that expression establishes.}
For this calculation, we consider a task AI-able if it is exposed to AI (exposure-based EFI).\footnote{We count as AI-exposed both E1- and E2-exposed tasks, which together account for 44\% of the tasks in the dataset; restricting attention to E1-exposed tasks alone (14\% of tasks) would leave potential AI chains too sparse for fragmentation to be meaningfully measured.}
For robustness, we also consider an alternative measure that treats AI-executed tasks as AI-able (execution-based EFI).

We estimate the regression in Equation~\eqref{eq:fragmentation_index_regression} using the exposure-based EFI.

\subsection{Prediction \#3: Adjacency to AI Tasks Increases Likelihood of AI Execution}
\label{sec:DWA_prediction}

The final prediction of the model that we test is that when the same step appears in two occupations, in one sitting between strong AI performers and in the other located between manual ones, it is more likely to be executed by AI in the first occupation.
Intuitively, when a step is surrounded by AI-executed steps, there are local gains from grouping it with its neighbors as part of a long AI chain that are absent when the step is situated next to manual steps.

Because O*NET tasks are occupation-specific, no single task is shared across occupations in the data.
To work around this, we rely on O*NET's broader categories of Detailed Work Activities (DWAs), which group conceptually similar tasks across occupations.
In our main sample, we drop tasks that are mapped to multiple DWAs.
We further restrict attention to DWAs that contain tasks appearing in more than one occupation.

This implies that local work context and proximity does work that the reshuffled orderings cannot reproduce by chance.

Overall, the results indicate that AI execution of immediate neighbors of the focal task increases the likelihood that the task is executed by AI, whereas execution status of more distant neighbors matters much less, especially after controlling for a range of relevant factors.
This aligns with the relatively short average AI chain length observed in the data (mean length 1.45), implying that tasks two positions away rarely fall in the same chain as the focal task.
It is possible that with improvements in AI quality, chains become longer and execution status of more distant neighbors becomes more relevant for the execution mode of the focal task.


\subsection{Robustness Tests}
\label{sec:robustness}

Each of the three tests above is defined over adjacent steps, so all of them rest on the task ordering rather than on task content alone.
Two features of how we obtain that ordering warrant scrutiny, and we address each with a separate exercise.

The first concern is the prompt.
A single prompt produces a single set of sequences, so if the phrasing of our request is what organizes the tasks, the resulting ordering might be a property of our instructions rather than of the work itself.
In Appendix~\ref{app:gptPrompts_robustness} we re-generate the O*NET task sequences under ten alternative prompts that vary the framing and the ordering logic, and re-run all three tests on each of the resulting datasets.
The estimates move within a narrow band, and none of the conclusions reported above turns on which prompt is used.
This shows that our results are not artifacts of the specific prompt or the sequencing logic used to order tasks.

The second concern is the taxonomy of the O*NET dataset itself.
O*NET catalogues all tasks an occupation involves, including those that are rarely performed, and such tasks might act as ``filler'' steps that lengthen a workflow and separate steps that are adjacent in practice.
In Appendix~\ref{app:frequency_robustness} we restrict the sample to progressively more frequently-executed tasks, which makes the surviving sequences more workflow-like, and recover the same patterns for all three predictions.
Confidence intervals widen as the restriction shrinks the sample, since dropping infrequent tasks also removes AI-executed ones, which are already scarce to begin with, but the direction of the headline results remains unaffected.
This raises our confidence that our results do not hinge on the specific way that O*NET provides its occupation taxonomy.

The two robustness tests above, however, still rely on LLM-generated orderings, so neither can establish that the imputed order corresponds to how work steps are actually sequenced.
To validate our LLM sequencing approach externally, in Appendix~\ref{app:external_validation} we apply it to two corpora of production processes whose orderings come from actual work practice rather than from a language model.
The first is the Process Classification Framework (PCF) of the American Productivity \& Quality Center (APQC), whose member working groups have recorded the activities and tasks composing a firm's business processes in the order practitioners perform them \citep{apqc_pcf}.
The second is a collection of process-mining event logs from the 4TU.ResearchData repository \citep{roadfines_log, sepsis_log, bpi2017_log, bpi2020_log}, covering police, hospital, banking, and university administrative processes, where the order of work is observed in the recorded traces rather than documented after the fact.
In both cases we shuffle the steps of a process and ask the LLM to restore their order, under our main sequencing prompt and each of the ten alternatives.
The LLM recovers a large majority of the benchmark precedences in both corpora, the documented and the observed benchmark agree closely with each other, and neither result moves much across prompts.
The overlap is substantial rather than exact, which is what one would expect of a procedure that infers an order from task descriptions alone, but it is systematic enough that our imputed sequences cannot be read as an artifact of the instrument.

Having established that the LLM can reasonably recover the ordering of production activities, we then match O*NET tasks to PCF activities using natural language processing techniques to carry the AI exposure and execution labels over to this practitioner-ordered corpus, and re-test our predictions there.
Although not every AI-exposed or AI-executed task is matched to an activity in the PCF corpus, Predictions~\#1 and~\#2 come through with essentially the same results: AI-executed steps co-occur far more than the same labels arranged at random along the same sequences, and the empirical fragmentation index coefficient stays negative and significant at a magnitude comparable to the main sample.
We are unable to re-run Prediction~\#3, however, whose sample requirements are considerably more restrictive, since it needs the same step to recur across PCF workflows with differing execution outcomes and the O*NET-PCF matched data does not retain enough of that variation.

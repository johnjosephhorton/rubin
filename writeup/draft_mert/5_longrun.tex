So far we held the workflow content of the worker fixed, and assumed AI could improve only the time that took to execute those steps. 
The results of Section~\ref{sec:results} thus followed from only that one margin.
The more consequential effects of AI, however, arrive when it does more than make a worker faster at the work they have been assigned.
Once a firm is also able to change the allocation of steps to workers, it fully restructures its production around the capabilities of AI.
That is inherently a longer-run object. 
In a longer time horizon, the firm decides jointly where to deploy AI along the workflow and where to divide it between workers.
These decisions cannot be separated, as changing the task assignment of workers changes the skill requirements of jobs and thus will affect wages.

Here we develop the firm's long run problem.
Section~\ref{sec:longrun.jobdesign} lets the firm apportion the workflow across several workers, which requires endogenizing wages, and states the joint problem over AI deployment and job design that results.
Section~\ref{sec:longrun.specialization} then discusses what stops the firm from dividing the workflow indefinitely, and shows how the answer to that question changes once AI is deployed.
Appendix~\ref{app:jobdesign} carries the problem further and relates the resulting trade-off to the literature on the division of labor within the firm.


\subsection{Jobs}
\label{sec:longrun.jobdesign}

A job is a subsequence of tasks that are assigned to a single human worker.
A job $J$ is a contiguous subsequence of tasks, say $J = (T_b, \dotsc, T_{b+\ell})$ for some $b$ and $\ell$.
We say that a partition $\mathcal{J} = (J_1, \dotsc, J_p)$ of all tasks into jobs is a \emph{job design}.
Figure~\ref{fig:hierarchical_production} extends the example of Figure~\ref{fig:steps_tasks} to include this additional layer of bundling tasks into jobs.

Completing a step demands not only time but also skill, by which we mean the body of knowledge and the specific know-how a worker must acquire in order to perform it.
The short run had no need of this notion, as we assumed workers had acquired skills necessary for to perform all activities in their job. 
In the long run, however, the firm chooses which steps to assign to which worker and thus we introduce skills to capture the changes in requirements of jobs. 

Accordingly, we extend Definitions~\ref{def:manual_task}--\ref{def:ai_chain} to incorporate the skill cost of steps as well: performing step $i$ manually costs $(\manualSkill{i},\, \manualTime{i})$, and managing one AI attempt at it costs $(\AIskill{i},\, \AItime{i})$.
The two costs need not coincide, since doing the work oneself calls for knowing how to do it, whereas directing an AI to do it and judging what comes back may call for knowledge of a different kind.
As with time costs, a task inherits the skill of whichever mode executes it, so each task $T_b$ in the task sequence $\T = \{T_1, \dotsc, T_n\}$ carries a pair of skill and time costs $(\skillcost{b}, \timecost{b})$ that depend on whether it is completed manually or with the aid of AI.
An AI chain therefore demands only the skill required to check and validate the output of its augmented step, and an automated step inside a chain demands no skill of anyone, since no human ever handles it directly.

The firm hires one worker for each job in its job design $\mathcal{J}$.
The worker for job $J_j$ completes all tasks associated with their job.
The total time needed to complete all tasks in job $J_j$ is $\sum_{T_b \in J_j} \timecost{b}$.

A worker's wage is determined by the total skill required to complete their job.
The total skill required to complete the tasks in job $J_j$ is $\sum_{T_b \in J_j} \skillcost{b}$.
We then assume that a worker's wage is proportional to the skill level of their job.\footnote{
One motivation for this formulation is workers paying a human capital investment to acquire the skills necessary for a job.
This investment must be offset by wages, and we express skill levels in units of their corresponding requisite wage.
Rather than formalizing such a wage model here, we impose this as an assumption and provide a more detailed wage formulation in Appendix~\ref{sec:aggregation}.
}
That is,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:wage}
\text{Wage}_j = \sum_{T_b \in J_j} \skillcost{b}.
\end{align}

The firm must pay workers their wage per unit of time regardless of which tasks they are assigned to complete at any given moment.\footnote{
This derivation implicitly assumes a common base wage rate; i.e., all wage differentiation is driven by the human capital cost of acquiring skills.
More generally, different tasks $T_b$ within job $J_j$ could have different base wage rates $w_b$ that could be influenced, for example, by the supply of and demand for labor of the corresponding type.
One special case, explicitly motivated and discussed in Appendix~\ref{sec:aggregation}, assumes manual tasks are executed by human labor at base wage rate $w_{\manualLetter}$, and AI-assisted tasks are executed by AI management labor at base wage rate $w_{\AIletter}$.
The formulation in Equation~\eqref{eq:wage} implicitly normalizes these base wage rates to $1$, incorporating them into skill costs $\skillcost{b}$ for notational simplicity.
We maintain this normalization throughout subsequent sections until we explicitly distinguish human labor and AI management labor base wage rates in Appendix~\ref{sec:aggregation}.
\label{foot:wage}
}
Taking into account both time and skill requirements, the total wage bill paid to a worker assigned to job $J_j$, per unit of output, is
\begin{align}
\label{eq:jobcost}
\text{WageBill}_j = \Biggl(\sum_{T_b \in J_j} \skillcost{b} \Biggr)\Biggl(\sum_{T_b \in J_j} \timecost{b} \Biggr).
\end{align}
Note what \eqref{eq:jobcost} implies for a job.
The worker is compensated for every skill the job demands over its whole duration, even though at any given moment they are performing a single task and drawing on only the portion of their knowledge that task requires.
The remainder of their human capital sits idle at that moment and is paid for regardless.

Absent any frictions, the firm would minimize production costs by specializing as far as it can.
Dividing a job in two splits its skill requirement between two workers while leaving the total time to be worked unchanged, so it strictly lowers the wage bill.
Carried to its conclusion, the firm would fully specialize work and assign every task to its own worker, each of whom acquires exactly the knowledge their task requires and none of whom is ever paid for skill they are not using.

In reality, work is not organized this way, because dividing production among many workers is not free.
Each division creates a point at which work must pass from one person to the next, and coordinating that intermediate output takes time: whoever receives it has to be told what was done, why, and what state the output is now in, and whoever hands it over has to put it in a form that can be received.
We model this coordination friction with an additional \emph{hand-off cost}, in time, that a worker spends passing their work on to the next worker, incurred whenever $J_j$ is not the final job in the job design $\mathcal{J}$.

Because our production process has an order, we can be more specific than a cost that depends only on how many workers there are.
A hand-off happens at a particular point in production, and what it costs depends on which point.
Some places are natural seams, where the work has reached a self-contained intermediate output, a finished design or a signed-off specification, that can be described briefly and passed on.
Others fall in the middle of something, where whoever receives the work has to reconstruct the context the previous worker had built up.
We therefore attach the hand-off cost to the step at which the break occurs rather than to the worker or to the number of jobs, so that \emph{where} the firm divides the workflow matters and not merely how often it divides it.
The red curly arrows in Figure~\ref{fig:hierarchical_production} illustrate how hand-offs occur at job boundaries and that this cost reflects the characteristics of the last step in a worker's job.

Formally, given step $s_i$, we write $\handofftime{i}$ for the additional hand-off time spent by a worker for whom the last step of their job is $s_i$, and $\handofftime{}(J_j)$ for the hand-off time of job $J_j$, which is equal to $\handofftime{i}$ if $s_i$ is the final step in $J_j$.
The final job hands off to no one, so $\handofftime{m} = 0$.
Conditional on where the hand-off occurs in the production process, its cost does not otherwise depend on previous hand-off events.
Thus the total time needed to complete job $J_j$, including hand-off costs, is
\[
\handofftime{}(J_j) + \sum_{T_b \in J_j} \timecost{b}.
\]

Because the worker is paid for as long as their job takes, this time enters the wage bill exactly as execution time does, so \eqref{eq:jobcost} becomes
\begin{align}
\label{eq:wagebill}
\text{WageBill}_j = \Biggl(\sum_{T_b \in J_j} \skillcost{b} \Biggr) \Biggl(\handofftime{}(J_j) + \sum_{T_b \in J_j} \timecost{b} \Biggr).
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:wagebill} highlights two opposing forces that shape a job's boundaries.
Adding more tasks to a worker's job increases the cumulative skill requirement and thus the wage rate for that worker.
However, if tasks are kept separate, workers must incur additional hand-off costs at each job boundary.
These two effects, higher wages from combining tasks versus higher hand-off costs from keeping them separate, jointly determine the optimal degree of task aggregation.
We explore this trade-off in greater detail in Section~\ref{sec:longrun.specialization}.

Given the sequence of steps $\mathcal{S} = \{s_1, \dotsc, s_m\}$, the firm can design both the partition $\T$ of steps into tasks and the partition $\mathcal{J}$ of tasks into jobs.
The choice of $\T$ determines the time and skill requirements of each task.
Given $\T$, the choice of $\mathcal{J}$ then determines worker wage rates and time needed per unit of output (including hand-off costs).
Formally, we can write $\mathcal{P}(X)$ for the set of partitions of a sequence $X$ into contiguous subsequences.
Then the full optimization problem faced by the firm can be expressed as
\begin{align}
\label{eq:totalcost_with_handoff}
\min_{\T \in \mathcal{P}(\mathcal{S})} \
\min_{\mathcal{J} \in \mathcal{P}(\T)} \
\text{TotalCost}(\mathcal{J}; \T)
= \sum_{J_j \in \mathcal{J}} \text{WageBill}_j =
\sum_{J_j \in \mathcal{J}} \Biggl[\Biggl(\sum_{T_b \in J_j} \skillcost{b} \Biggr) \Biggl(\handofftime{}(J_j) + \sum_{T_b \in J_j} \timecost{b} \Biggr)\Biggr]
\end{align}
where recall that, for each $T_b \in \T$, $\timecost{b}$ is as described in Section~\ref{sec:model.tasks} and $\skillcost{b}$ as above, both depending on the selected mode of operation for individual steps.

We refer to \eqref{eq:totalcost_with_handoff} as the firm's \emph{long-run} optimization problem, as it anticipates the adjustment of worker wages to fit the skill requirements of each job and sets job responsibilities accordingly.
It nests the short-run problem \eqref{eq:totalcost_shortterm} of Section~\ref{sec:jobs_wages} as the case of a single job spanning the whole workflow, whose wage is fixed rather than chosen: with the job design given, minimizing the wage bill reduces to minimizing the time the job takes, and the firm optimizes each job separately.
Section~\ref{sec:extensions.computation} shows that \eqref{eq:totalcost_with_handoff} can be solved to any desired accuracy in polynomial time.
Panel~B of Table~\ref{tab:model_parameters} collects the notation this section adds.


\subsection{Hand-off Costs and the Limits of Worker Specialization}
\label{sec:longrun.specialization}

What the firm settles when it solves \eqref{eq:totalcost_with_handoff} is how far to carry the division of labor, and the answer is interior precisely because hand-offs are costly.
This subsection makes that limit concrete.
We work through a numerical example and its geometric counterpart, and then ask how AI moves the balance the example strikes.

Consider a workflow that, under a fixed AI deployment strategy $\T$, has $n=3$ tasks with the following cost parameters:
\[ \bigl(\skillcost{b},\, \timecost{b},\, \handofftime{b}\bigr)_{b=1,2,3} = (3,\,1,\,3),\quad (1,\,2,\,0.5),\quad (2,\,2,\,0). \]
With three tasks there are four ways the firm can design jobs.\footnote{
More generally, a production sequence with $n$ tasks has $2^{(n-1)}$ unique job designs.
}
Denoting jobs by square brackets, they are
$\{[1][2][3], \quad [1,2][3], \quad [1][2,3], \quad [1,2,3]\}$.

Panel (b) reinstates them, and the optimum moves to $[1,2][3]$, in which tasks 1 and 2 are bundled and task 3 stands alone.
The reason is the large hand-off time $\handofftime{1}=3$ that a boundary between tasks 1 and 2 would trigger.
Bundling them buys that hand-off away at the price of a worker who must hold the skills of both, and who is paid for both throughout.
Equation~\eqref{eq:wagebill} is what makes the comparison: bundling raises the wage through $\sum_{T_b \in J_j} \skillcost{b}$, and splitting raises time through $\handofftime{}(J_j)$.
Neither force wins outright, and where they balance is the firm's degree of specialization.

In Panel~(a) a design costs exactly the area its jobs enclose, and splitting always shrinks that area, since each new boundary removes the dead space a worker's idle skill creates while they work on a task that does not need it.
Panel~(b) adds the pink hand-off rectangles, and their heights are what decide the comparison: a hand-off rectangle is as tall as the entire skill requirement of the worker departing at that boundary, so a boundary drawn after a high-skill task is expensive in a way that one drawn after a low-skill task is not.
That is why the optimum here keeps tasks 1 and 2 together.
The boundary that would separate them falls after the most skilled task in the workflow and carries the longest hand-off, so bundling buys away nine units of cost, the skill of $3$ that task 1 demands held over the three units of time its hand-off takes.
The dead space bundling creates in exchange is smaller, chiefly the two units of time during task 2 in which the worker's skill of $3$ from task 1 goes unused.

AI moves this balance, and it does so through the skill margin rather than the time margin that Section~\ref{sec:results} worked with.
A chain replaces a run of steps with a single verification at its endpoint, so it strips out the skills the automated steps used to demand and leaves the worker holding only the skill needed to manage and check the chain.
That lowers the wage rate a bundled job must pay, and with it the penalty from bundling, making jobs that contain more tasks cheaper to sustain: the same coordination cost now buys more.
With a fixed set of production steps, this means that one worker's job extends to absorb the tasks of adjacent workers.
Automation and job consolidation are therefore complements in our framework, which is the sense in which AI redefines work rather than merely displacing it from a fixed set of jobs.

Working in the opposite direction, we treat hand-off costs as fixed, whereas AI plausibly lowers them too, by absorbing the peripheral coordination work of a transfer such as summarizing prior work or drafting the documentation that accompanies it.
Were that channel active, it would push toward narrower jobs and more specialization, so the net effect of AI on job breadth is an empirical question our framework poses rather than settles.\footnote{
A useful reading of the hand-off cost is that it has two components: peripheral coordination work that AI can facilitate, and a remainder that rests on tacit, interpersonal, or social knowledge and is not easily executable by AI \citep{deming2017growing}.
The hand-off cost in the model should be read as the second, irreducibly human component.
}

\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Production is a sequence of steps that can be executed (1) manually, (2) augmented with AI, or (3) fully automated within contiguous AI-executed steps called ``chains.''
We characterize how a firm optimally assigns humans and AI across the steps of its workflow, and show that AI chaining can overturn comparative advantage logic: a step at which humans hold an advantage in isolation may still be automated when it sits beside strong AI-performing steps, because chaining removes the human verification checkpoint between them.
The model implies that the productivity gains from AI depend not only on which steps are exposed to AI but on where those steps sit in the workflow, and that marginal improvements in AI quality can generate non-monotone productivity gains as longer chains become viable.
Empirical evidence supports the model's key predictions that (1) AI-executed steps co-occur in chains, (2) dispersion of AI-exposed steps lowers AI execution at the workflow level, and (3) adjacency to AI-executed steps increases the likelihood that a step is AI-executed.
We then embed this chaining problem in the firm's longer-run redesign of jobs around AI, and show that firm-level production aggregates to a macroeconomic CES production function.

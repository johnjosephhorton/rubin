So far we assumed a single worker executes the entire workflow, and that AI could improve only the time that took to execute steps. 
The results of Section~\ref{sec:results} thus followed from only that one margin, reflecting the short-run effects of deploying AI.
The more consequential effects of AI, however, arrive when it does more than make a worker faster at the work they have been assigned.
Once a firm is also able to change the allocation of steps to workers and fully restructure production around capabilities of AI by adjusting how work steps are apportioned across workers rather than only the pace at which each person's share is executed the real gains from AI are realized.
That is inherently a longer-run object: the firm decides jointly where to deploy AI along the workflow and where to divide it between workers, and the two decisions cannot be separated, since automating a run of steps changes what the worker who owns them has to be able to do.

This section develops that problem and closes the model in two steps.
First, we let the firm apportion the workflow across several workers, which requires two ingredients the short-run problem ignored: endogenizing wages, and considering costs of assigning bundles of tasks to workers.
Second, we show that individual firms' production functions resulting from the optimization problem we introduce can be represented by a single macro production function across the economy, showing that a workflow-level technology with endogenous task and job boundaries is consistent with a single aggregate production function at the macro level.
We keep both at the level of the economic content here; the full characterizations are developed in Appendices~\ref{app:jobdesign} and~\ref{sec:aggregation}.


\subsection{Job Design and the Limits of Specialization}
\label{sec:longrun.jobdesign}

Rather than assigning the whole workflow to a single worker, the firm may divide it and hire several.
Just as an AI strategy partitions the step sequence $\mathcal{S}$ into a sequence of contiguous tasks $\T$, the firm partitions that task sequence into contiguous blocks, each of which it assigns to a single worker.
We call each such block a \emph{job} and the partition $\J = (J_1, \dotsc, J_p)$ of $\T$ into $p$ jobs a \emph{job design}.
The problem of Section~\ref{sec:jobs_wages} is the special case of a job design with a single job spanning the whole workflow.

How finely the firm should divide its workflow is not obvious, and the answer turns on a tension between two forces.
A worker responsible for many tasks must be capable of performing all of them, and breadth of that kind does not come free.
Dividing the workflow into narrower jobs lets each worker be responsible for less, and so cost the firm less, but production must then pass through more hands, and handing work from one person to the next consumes resources of its own.
The firm's job design settles this tension, and the rest of this subsection makes each side of it precise: first what breadth costs (i.e., wages), and then what dividing work costs.

In the long run, the firm deploys AI not only to raise productivity at individual steps, and hence at the tasks those steps compose, but also to change the allocation of tasks to workers.
Wages are no longer the pre-specified rate the firm committed to when it hired, and instead vary with what each job actually requires of the worker who holds it.
Wages and organizational structure, by which we mean which tasks are assigned to which workers, thus become margins the firm optimizes alongside its AI deployment strategy.

To capture this we associate with each step a cost beyond the time its execution takes, namely a \emph{skill cost}, which reflects the body of knowledge and the specific know-how a worker must acquire in order to perform that step.
We extend Definitions~\ref{def:manual_task} and~\ref{def:augmented_task} accordingly: performing step $i$ manually costs $(\manualSkill{i},\, \manualTime{i})$, and managing one AI attempt at it costs $(\AIskill{i},\, \AItime{i})$.
The two need not coincide, since doing the work oneself calls for knowing how to do it, whereas directing an AI to do it and judging what comes back may call for less knowledge, or for knowledge of a different kind.
A task inherits the skill of whichever mode executes it, so task $T_b$ carries skill $\skillcost{b}$, equal to $\manualSkill{i}$ if it is a manual step and to $\AIskill{r}$ if it is an AI chain augmented at step $r$.
An automated step inside a chain demands no skill of anyone, since no human ever handles it.
Similarly, the skill costs of an AI chain, is the skill required to check, and validate, the output of the augmented step. 
That is, the skill cost of an AI chain is the same as the skill cost of its augmented step.

Workers are ex-ante identical and are assigned to jobs by the firm.
A worker assigned to job $J_j$ acquires the skills needed to perform all of the job's tasks, and the firm compensates that training through the wage it pays them.
Because workers are identical and free to take any job, wages must leave them indifferent between sorting into different jobs: a job that obliges its holder to acquire more skill must pay correspondingly more to cover the additional training it requires.
We thus take the wage rate of a job to be the flow rate at which the firm compensates a worker for the skills that job obliged them to acquire.

Since skills are acquired separately for each individual task, the job's overall compensation is additive over skill cost of all tasks the job contains,\footnote{
We express skill levels directly in units of the wage they command, which is what lets the skill a task requires and the compensation it commands be written as the same object.
Rather than formalize the underlying human capital problem here, we impose \eqref{eq:wage} as an assumption and develop a more detailed formulation in Appendix~\ref{sec:aggregation}.
}
\begin{align}
\label{eq:wage}
\text{Wage}_j = \sum_{T_b \in J_j} \skillcost{b},
\end{align}
and the firm pays this rate per unit of time for as long as the job takes, regardless of which task the worker is performing at any given moment.\footnote{
This implicitly assumes a common base wage rate, so that all wage differences are driven by the human capital cost of acquiring skills.
More generally, manual and AI-assisted tasks could carry different base wage rates $w_{\manualLetter}$ and $w_{\AIletter}$, reflecting the supply of and demand for each type of labor; Equation~\eqref{eq:wage} normalizes these rates to $1$ and folds them into the skill costs $\skillcost{b}$.
We maintain this normalization for notational simplicity but it can be relaxed to more general cases.
\label{foot:wage}
}
Equation~\eqref{eq:wage} is the object the short run held fixed and normalized to $1$, and it is now something the firm chooses.

Since the worker is paid this rate per unit of time for the entire duration of their job, the labor cost of job $J_j$, its \emph{wage bill}, is its wage rate times the time it takes,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:jobcost}
\text{WageBill}_j = \Biggl(\sum_{T_b \in J_j} \skillcost{b} \Biggr)\Biggl(\sum_{T_b \in J_j} \timecost{b} \Biggr).
\end{align}
Note what this implies for a broad job.
The worker is compensated for every skill the job demands over its whole duration, even though at any given moment they are performing a single task and drawing on only the portion of their knowledge that task requires.
The remainder of their human capital sits idle at that moment and is paid for regardless.

In a world without frictions the firm would respond by specializing as far as it can.
Dividing a job in two splits its skill requirement between two workers while leaving the total time to be worked unchanged, so it strictly lowers the wage bill.
Carried to its conclusion, the firm would assign every step to its own worker, each of whom acquires exactly the knowledge their step requires and none of whom is ever paid for skill they are not using.

Work is not organized this way, because dividing production among many workers is not free.
Each division creates a point at which work in progress must pass from one person to the next, and coordinating that intermediate output takes time: whoever receives it has to be told what was done, why, and what state the output is now in, and whoever hands it over has to put it in a form that can be received.
We capture this friction with a \emph{hand-off cost}, the additional time a worker spends passing their work on to the next worker.
For Adam Smith's pin factory the limit on specialization was the extent of the market; for the sequential knowledge work we have in mind it is this coordination cost, which is the friction that bounds specialization in \citet{becker1992division} and the reason the skills used to carry out such transfers, which \citet{deming2017growing} shows have become increasingly well paid, bear on how work can be organized at all.

Because our production process is a sequence, we can be more specific than a cost that depends only on how many workers there are.
A hand-off happens at a particular point in the workflow, and what it costs depends on which point.
Some places are natural seams, where the work has reached a self-contained intermediate output, a finished design or a signed-off specification, that can be described briefly and passed on.
Others fall in the middle of something, where whoever receives the work has to reconstruct the context the previous worker had built up.
Handing a completed design to an engineer is cheap; stopping halfway through the design and handing over the fragments is not.
We therefore attach the hand-off cost to the step at which the break occurs rather than to the worker or to the number of jobs, so that \emph{where} the firm divides the workflow matters and not merely how often it divides it.

We write $\handofftime{i}$ for the time a hand-off takes when step $s_i$ is the last step of a job, and $\handofftime{}(J_j)$ for the hand-off time of job $J_j$; the final job hands off to no one, so $\handofftime{m} = 0$.
Because the worker is paid for as long as their job takes, this time enters the wage bill exactly as execution time does, so \eqref{eq:jobcost} becomes
\begin{align}
\label{eq:lr_wagebill}
\text{WageBill}_j = \Biggl(\sum_{T_b \in J_j} \skillcost{b} \Biggr) \Biggl(\handofftime{}(J_j) + \sum_{T_b \in J_j} \timecost{b} \Biggr).
\end{align}

Equation~\eqref{eq:lr_wagebill} contains the whole trade-off, with one factor for each of the two forces above.
Bundling tasks into one job raises the first, for the reason already given: the worker must be skilled enough for all of them and is paid that rate throughout.
Splitting them raises the second, since every additional boundary inserts another hand-off.
Figure~\ref{fig:lr_job_design} makes this geometric: a job's wage bill is the area of a rectangle whose height is the skill it requires and whose width is the time it takes, and the firm is choosing how to cover the workflow with such rectangles.

In the long run the firm chooses the AI strategy and the job design together,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:lr_totalcost}
\min_{\T} \ \min_{\J} \
\sum_{J_j \in \J} \Biggl[\Biggl(\sum_{T_b \in J_j} \skillcost{b} \Biggr) \Biggl(\handofftime{}(J_j) + \sum_{T_b \in J_j} \timecost{b} \Biggr)\Biggr],
\end{align}
which nests the fixed-boundary problem \eqref{eq:totalcost_mediumrun} of Appendix~\ref{app:mediumrun_dp} as the case of a single job spanning the whole workflow.
Appendix~\ref{app:jobdesign.longrun} shows that this joint problem remains solvable to any desired accuracy.

The economics of putting the two margins together is that AI acts on job design through the skill dimension, not only the time dimension.
A chain replaces a run of steps with a single verification at its endpoint, so it strips out the skills the automated steps used to demand and leaves the worker holding only the skill needed to manage and check the chain.
That shrinks the height of the rectangle a bundled job must cover, which lowers the wage penalty from bundling and makes wider jobs cheaper to sustain: the same coordination cost now buys more.
Automation and consolidation of jobs are therefore complements in our framework, which is the sense in which AI redefines work rather than merely displacing it from a fixed set of jobs.
Working in the opposite direction, we treat hand-off costs as fixed, whereas AI plausibly lowers them too, by absorbing the peripheral coordination work of a transfer such as summarizing prior work or drafting the documentation that accompanies it.
Were that channel active, it would push toward narrower jobs and more specialization, so the net effect of AI on job breadth is an empirical question our framework poses rather than settles.\footnote{
A useful reading of the hand-off cost is that it has two components: peripheral coordination work that AI can facilitate, and a remainder that rests on tacit, interpersonal, or social knowledge and is not easily executable by AI \citep{deming2017growing}.
The hand-off cost in the model should be read as the second, irreducibly human component.
}
Appendix~\ref{app:jobdesign} develops the full problem, including a worked example in which hand-off costs overturn full specialization, and Appendix~\ref{sec:model_discussion} relates the resulting trade-off to the division of labor literature.


\subsection{From the Firm to the Macroeconomy}
\label{sec:longrun.aggregation}

Our production technology is Leontief over the steps of a workflow, with the boundaries of tasks and jobs chosen by the firm.
This is a more detailed object than the aggregate production functions used to study automation at the macro level, and it is natural to ask whether the two are compatible: does a workflow-level technology with endogenous task boundaries aggregate to anything a macroeconomist would recognize?
It does, in two steps, and the answer tells us where the firm's organizational choices show up once the economy is viewed in aggregate.

The first step collapses the workflow.
For a given AI strategy and job design, every task is executed either by AI management labor or by manual labor, and the many-input Leontief function over individual tasks reduces to a Leontief function over just those two aggregates,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:lr_micro_prod}
x = \min\left\{ \frac{\bar{\alpha}\,\labor{\AIletter}}{\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\AIletter}}, \ \frac{\labor{\manualLetter}}{\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\manualLetter}} \right\},
\end{align}
where $\labor{\AIletter}$ and $\labor{\manualLetter}$ are the firm's AI management and manual labor, and $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\AIletter}$ and $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\manualLetter}$ are the skill-adjusted time requirements of the two groups of tasks, with the hand-off times of Section~\ref{sec:longrun.jobdesign} entering $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\manualLetter}$.
The organizational problem of the previous sections survives aggregation entirely inside these coefficients: which steps are chained, where jobs are cut, and how much verification the workflow requires all enter through $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\AIletter}$ and $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\manualLetter}$, and through the firm's \emph{effective} AI quality
\begin{align}
\label{eq:lr_alphabar}
\bar{\alpha} = \frac{\sum_{b}\skillAdjustedTimeAI{b}}{\sum_{b}\skillAdjustedTimeAI{b}\,\alpha^{-d_{b}}},
\end{align}
a time-weighted harmonic average of the success probabilities of the chains the firm actually runs.
Two firms facing the same general-purpose technology $\alpha$ therefore have different $\bar{\alpha}$ when they deploy it over different arrangements of work, which is the sense in which effective AI quality is an organizational object and not only a technological one.

The second step aggregates across firms.
Individual Leontief technologies do not aggregate to a smooth production function on their own; what smooths them is heterogeneity, in the tradition of \citet{houthakker1955pareto} and \citet{levhari1968note}.
Here the natural dimension of heterogeneity is the one the model already delivers: firms differ in how effectively they put the same AI technology to work, that is, in $\bar{\alpha}$.
Appendix~\ref{sec:aggregation} shows that there is a distribution of $\bar{\alpha}$ across firms under which the economy admits the CES representation
\begin{align}
\label{eq:lr_macro_ces}
X = \left(\theta_{\AIletter}\,\aggregateAIlabor^{\rho} + \theta_{\manualLetter}\,\aggregateManualLabor^{\rho} + (1-\theta_{\AIletter}-\theta_{\manualLetter})\,K^{\rho}\right)^{\frac{1}{\rho}},
\end{align}
over economy-wide AI management labor $\aggregateAIlabor$, manual labor $\aggregateManualLabor$, and capital $K$, and derives that distribution in closed form.
Firms whose effective AI quality is too low to cover the cost of managing AI stay fully manual, so the aggregates in \eqref{eq:lr_macro_ces} are taken over the firms above an endogenous adoption threshold, and improvements in $\alpha$ move both the threshold and the input coefficients of the firms already above it.

Two things follow that matter for how our results should be read.
The first is a licence: the CES production functions used to study AI and labor demand at the macro level are consistent with production being sequential and with task boundaries being chosen rather than given, so nothing in our framework requires abandoning that apparatus.
The second is a caution about what the apparatus conceals.
The chaining results of Section~\ref{sec:comparative_advantage} and the fragmentation result of Section~\ref{sec:fragmentation} operate on $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\AIletter}$, $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\manualLetter}$, and $\bar{\alpha}$, which appear in \eqref{eq:lr_macro_ces} only through the input aggregates and the CES weights.
Two economies with identical AI exposure at the step level, differing only in how those steps are arranged within workflows, therefore reach the aggregate production function with different coefficients while looking identical in any step-level exposure measure.
Aggregation preserves the arrangement of work, but it stores it in the parameters rather than displaying it, which is precisely why the fragmentation index has to be measured at the level of the workflow.

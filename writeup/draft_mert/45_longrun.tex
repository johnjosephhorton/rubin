So far we assumed a single worker executes the entire workflow, and that AI could improve only the time that took to execute steps. 
The results of Section~\ref{sec:results} thus followed from only that one margin.
The more consequential effects of AI, however, arrive when it does more than make a worker faster at the work they have been assigned.
Once a firm is also able to change the allocation of steps to workers, it fully restructures its production around the capabilities of AI.
That is inherently a longer-run object. 
In the long run, the firm decides jointly where to deploy AI along the workflow and where to divide it between workers, and the two decisions cannot be separated, since automating a run of steps changes what the worker who owns them has to be able to do.

This section develops the firm's long-run problem and closes the model in two steps.
First, we let the firm apportion the workflow across several workers while it decides on its AI deployment strategy, which requires endogenizing wages.
Second, we show that the production functions resulting from individual firms' cost optimization can be represented by a single macro production function across the economy, showing that a workflow-level technology with endogenous task and job boundaries is consistent with a single aggregate production function at the macro level.
We keep both at the level of the economic content here; the full characterizations are developed in Appendices~\ref{app:jobdesign} and~\ref{sec:aggregation}.


\subsection{Job Design and the Limits of Specialization}
\label{sec:longrun.jobdesign}

The firm can divide its production sequence and hire different workers to perform different contiguous blocks of tasks.
Just as an AI strategy partitions the step sequence $\mathcal{S}$ into a sequence of contiguous tasks $\T$, the firm can partition the task sequence into contiguous blocks, each of which it assigns to a single worker.
We call each such block a \emph{job} and the partition $\J = (J_1, \dotsc, J_p)$ of $\T$ into $p$ jobs a \emph{job design}.
The problem of Section~\ref{sec:jobs_wages} is the case of a job design with a single job spanning the whole workflow.
Figure~\ref{fig:hierarchical_production} extends the example of Figure~\ref{fig:steps_tasks} to include the additional layer firms face in the long-run optimization problem, the bundling of tasks into jobs.

How finely the firm should divide its workflow among workers is not obvious, and the answer turns on a tension between two forces.
A worker responsible for many tasks must be capable of performing all of them, and breadth of that kind commands a high wage.
Dividing the workflow into narrower jobs lets each worker be responsible for less, and so cost the firm less, but production must then pass through more hands, and handing work from one person to the next consumes resources of its own.
The firm's job design settles this tension, and the rest of this subsection makes each side of it precise.

In the long run, wages are no longer the pre-specified rate the firm had committed to before deploying AI, and are allowed to vary with what each job actually requires of the worker who holds it.
Wages and organizational structure, by which we mean which tasks are assigned to which workers, thus become endogenous margins the firm optimizes alongside its AI deployment strategy.

To capture this we associate with each step a cost beyond the time its execution takes, namely a \emph{skill cost}, which reflects the body of knowledge and the specific know-how a worker must acquire in order to perform that step.
We extend Definitions~\ref{def:manual_task}--\ref{def:ai_chain} accordingly: performing step $i$ manually costs $(\manualSkill{i},\, \manualTime{i})$, and managing one AI attempt at it costs $(\AIskill{i},\, \AItime{i})$.
The two need not coincide, since doing the work oneself calls for knowing how to do it, whereas directing an AI to do it and judging what comes back may call for knowledge of a different kind.
A task inherits the skill of whichever mode executes it, so task $T_b$ carries skill $\skillcost{b}$, equal to $\manualSkill{i}$ if it is a manual step and to $\AIskill{r}$ if it is an AI chain augmented at step $r$.
An AI chain therefore demands only the skill required to check and validate the output of its augmented step, and an automated step inside a chain demands no skill of anyone, since no human ever handles it.

Workers are ex-ante identical and are assigned to jobs by the firm.
A worker assigned to job $J_j$ acquires the skills needed to perform all of the job's tasks, and the firm compensates that training through the wage it pays them.
Because workers are identical and free to take any job, wages must leave them indifferent between sorting into different jobs: a job that obliges its holder to acquire more skill must pay correspondingly more to cover the additional training it requires.
We thus take the wage rate of a job to be the flow rate at which the firm compensates a worker for the skills that job obliged them to acquire.

Since skills are acquired separately for each individual task, the job's overall compensation is additive over skill cost of all tasks the job contains,\footnote{
We express skill levels directly in units of the wage they command, which is what lets the skill a task requires and the compensation it commands be written as the same object.
Rather than formalize the underlying human capital problem here, we impose \eqref{eq:wage} as an assumption and develop a more detailed formulation in Appendix~\ref{sec:aggregation}.
}
\begin{align}
\label{eq:wage}
\text{Wage}_j = \sum_{T_b \in J_j} \skillcost{b},
\end{align}
and the firm pays this rate per unit of time for as long as the job takes, regardless of which task the worker is performing at any given moment.\footnote{
This implicitly assumes a common base wage rate, so that all wage differences are driven by the human capital cost of acquiring skills.
More generally, manual and AI-assisted tasks could carry different base wage rates $w_{\manualLetter}$ and $w_{\AIletter}$, reflecting the supply of and demand for each type of labor; Equation~\eqref{eq:wage} normalizes these rates to $1$ and folds them into the skill costs $\skillcost{b}$.
We maintain this normalization for notational simplicity but it can be relaxed to more general cases.
\label{foot:wage}
}
Equation~\eqref{eq:wage} is the object the short run held fixed and normalized to $1$, and it is now something the firm chooses.

Since the worker is paid this rate per unit of time for the entire duration of their job, the labor cost of job $J_j$, its \emph{wage bill}, is its wage rate times the time it takes,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:jobcost}
\text{WageBill}_j = \Biggl(\sum_{T_b \in J_j} \skillcost{b} \Biggr)\Biggl(\sum_{T_b \in J_j} \timecost{b} \Biggr).
\end{align}
Note what this implies for a job.
The worker is compensated for every skill the job demands over its whole duration, even though at any given moment they are performing a single task and drawing on only the portion of their knowledge that task requires.
The remainder of their human capital sits idle at that moment and is paid for regardless.

In a world without frictions the firm would minimize production costs by specializing as far as it can.
Dividing a job in two splits its skill requirement between two workers while leaving the total time to be worked unchanged, so it strictly lowers the wage bill.
Carried to its conclusion, the firm would assign every task to its own worker, each of whom acquires exactly the knowledge their task requires and none of whom is ever paid for skill they are not using.

In reality, work is not organized this way, because dividing production among many workers is not free.
Each division creates a point at which work must pass from one person to the next, and coordinating that intermediate output takes time: whoever receives it has to be told what was done, why, and what state the output is now in, and whoever hands it over has to put it in a form that can be received.
We model this coordination friction with an additional \emph{hand-off cost}, in time, that a worker spends passing their work on to the next worker.
% For Adam Smith's pin factory the limit on specialization was the extent of the market; for the sequential knowledge work we have in mind it is this coordination cost, which is the friction that bounds specialization in \citet{becker1992division} and the reason the skills used to carry out such transfers, which \citet{deming2017growing} shows have become increasingly well paid, bear on how work can be organized at all.

Because our production process has an order, we can be more specific than a cost that depends only on how many workers there are.
A hand-off happens at a particular point in the workflow, and what it costs depends on which point.
Some places are natural seams, where the work has reached a self-contained intermediate output, a finished design or a signed-off specification, that can be described briefly and passed on.
Others fall in the middle of something, where whoever receives the work has to reconstruct the context the previous worker had built up.
We therefore attach the hand-off cost to the step at which the break occurs rather than to the worker or to the number of jobs, so that \emph{where} the firm divides the workflow matters and not merely how often it divides it.
The red curly arrows in Figure~\ref{fig:hierarchical_production} illustrate how hand-offs occur at job boundaries and can be traced back to the steps separating two consecutive jobs.

Formally, we write $\handofftime{i}$ for the time a hand-off takes when step $s_i$ is the last step of a job, and $\handofftime{}(J_j)$ for the hand-off time of job $J_j$; the final job hands off to no one, so $\handofftime{m} = 0$.
Because the worker is paid for as long as their job takes, this time enters the wage bill exactly as execution time does, so \eqref{eq:jobcost} becomes
\begin{align}
\label{eq:lr_wagebill}
\text{WageBill}_j = \Biggl(\sum_{T_b \in J_j} \skillcost{b} \Biggr) \Biggl(\handofftime{}(J_j) + \sum_{T_b \in J_j} \timecost{b} \Biggr).
\end{align}

Equation~\eqref{eq:lr_wagebill} contains the whole job design trade-off, with one factor for each of the two forces above.
Bundling tasks into one job raises the first, for the reason already given: the worker must be skilled enough to perform all of the job's tasks and is paid that rate throughout.
Splitting tasks raises the second, since every additional boundary inserts another hand-off.
Figure~\ref{fig:lr_job_design} provides a geometric representation of the job design problem: each rectangle represents a task, its height the task's skill cost and its width its time cost.
A job's wage bill is then the area of the rectangle whose height is the skill the job requires and whose width is the time it takes, and the firm is choosing how to cover the workflow with such rectangles so as to minimize total area.

In the long run the firm chooses its AI deployment strategy and job design together,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:lr_totalcost}
\min_{\T} \ \min_{\J} \
\sum_{J_j \in \J} \Biggl[\Biggl(\sum_{T_b \in J_j} \skillcost{b} \Biggr) \Biggl(\handofftime{}(J_j) + \sum_{T_b \in J_j} \timecost{b} \Biggr)\Biggr],
\end{align}
which nests the short-run problem \eqref{eq:totalcost_shortterm} of Section~\ref{sec:jobs_wages} as the case of a single job spanning the whole workflow whose wage is held fixed rather than chosen.
Appendix~\ref{app:jobdesign.longrun} shows that the solution to this joint problem can be approximated in polynomial time, up to an error term that can be made arbitrarily small.

Across both margins of the firm's long-run problem, AI lowers production costs not only by enabling productivity gains and lowering the execution time of steps, but also by changing the skill requirements of jobs in the job design problem.
A chain replaces a run of steps with a single verification at its endpoint, so it strips out the skills the automated steps used to demand and leaves the worker holding only the skill needed to manage and check the chain.
That lowers the wage rate a bundled job must pay, and with it the penalty from bundling, making jobs that contain more tasks cheaper to sustain: the same coordination cost now buys more.
With a fixed set of production steps, this means that one worker's job extends to absorb the tasks of adjacent workers.
Automation and job consolidation are therefore complements in our framework, which is the sense in which AI redefines work rather than merely displacing it from a fixed set of jobs.
Working in the opposite direction, we treat hand-off costs as fixed, whereas AI plausibly lowers them too, by absorbing the peripheral coordination work of a transfer such as summarizing prior work or drafting the documentation that accompanies it.
Were that channel active, it would push toward narrower jobs and more specialization, so the net effect of AI on job breadth is an empirical question our framework poses rather than settles.\footnote{
A useful reading of the hand-off cost is that it has two components: peripheral coordination work that AI can facilitate, and a remainder that rests on tacit, interpersonal, or social knowledge and is not easily executable by AI \citep{deming2017growing}.
The hand-off cost in the model should be read as the second, irreducibly human component.
}
Appendix~\ref{app:jobdesign} develops the full problem, including a worked example in which hand-off costs overturn full specialization, and Appendix~\ref{sec:model_discussion} relates the resulting trade-off to the division of labor literature.


\subsection{From the Firm to the Macroeconomy}
\label{sec:longrun.aggregation}

So far the model has been about decision-making inside a single firm: how it allocates steps among manual execution, augmentation, and automation, and how it bundles the resulting tasks into jobs.
Our interest, however, extends beyond the firm.
Improvements in AI quality change not only how one firm organizes its production but also the composition of labor employed across the economy and the aggregate relationship between labor, capital, and output, and it is in those terms that the consequences of AI are usually debated.
The framework developed so far does not deliver them.
It also sets our approach apart from the task-based literature, which writes production functions directly at the level of the economy and studies automation there \citep{acemoglu2018, acemoglu2022artificial, acemoglu2024tasks}.
This subsection therefore asks whether the two are compatible: can the firm-level cost minimization we have posed be aggregated into a well-behaved production function for the economy, and if so, what becomes of the organizational detail along the way?

Imagine the economy employs two kinds of labor.
\emph{Manual labor} performs steps directly, and \emph{AI management labor} prompts the AI and verifies what it returns.
Every task in the model draws on one or the other, and an automated step inside a chain draws on neither, since no human handles it.
These are the two inputs any aggregate account of AI and work has to keep track of, and the obstacle is that our firms cannot trade one for the other.
Producing a unit of output requires completing every step of the workflow, so a firm's technology is Leontief: giving it more AI management labor without also giving it more manual labor buys nothing.
Aggregate production functions, by contrast, assume the two kinds of labor substitute, and the elasticity with which they do so carries most of the weight in any comparative static.
If no individual firm can substitute, where would aggregate substitution come from?

The answer, which goes back to \citet{houthakker1955pareto} and \citet{levhari1968note}, is that it can come entirely from composition.
Suppose the economy consists of many firms, and even though these firms have access to the same technology they differ in how much they can use it effectively.
When AI management labor becomes relatively cheaper, no firm changes its input mix, because none can.
What happens instead is that firms for which AI was previously not worth managing begin to use it, and the economy's input ratio moves because the set of firms for which using AI is beneficial has moved.
Rigid technologies plus dispersion across firms deliver a smooth aggregate, provided the dispersion is of the right kind.

Appendix~\ref{sec:aggregation} obtains dispersion of this kind from the order in which a firm has to commit.
Production unfolds in two stages.
In the first, the firm settles on an AI deployment strategy $\T$ and a job design $\J$ and posts vacancies to hire workers accordingly; in the second it hires, begins producing, and only then learns how effectively it can put AI to use.
In other words, a firm does not know its own effective AI quality when it is organizing its workflow, and all firms share a common prior about the distribution that quality is drawn from, so they make identical organizational choices.
When the time comes to actually produce the output, they draw different realizations of how effectively they are able to deploy AI, and by that point the organization is settled and cannot be adjusted to the draw.
This mirrors a realistic order in which firms actually behave: they structure operations and post vacancies before they discover how productive their inputs turn out to be.

\paragraph{Carrying out the aggregation.}
The exercise itself proceeds in two steps: we first collapse a single firm's workflow into a technology in two inputs, and then aggregate that technology across firms.
Let us fix the AI strategy $\T$ and job design $\J$ that firms have committed to, and assume that hand-offs are performed manually.
Producing a unit of output then requires completing every task in the workflow, and every hand-off between the workers who share it.
Each of these needs labor of its own: a manual task needs a worker who can perform it, an AI chain needs a worker who can prompt for it and check what comes back, and a hand-off needs time from the worker making it.
None of these substitutes for any other, since output requires all of them, so more labor on one task with no more on the rest produces nothing extra.
The firm's technology is therefore Leontief with one input per task and one per hand-off, and output is governed by whichever of them is least well supplied.

This many-input production function can be collapsed into a two-input one.
Because each requirement is proportional to output, the amounts of labor the tasks call for stand in fixed ratios to one another, so tasks drawing on the same kind of labor can be pooled without loss into a single input.
Let $\labor{\AIletter}$ and $\labor{\manualLetter}$ denote the quantities of AI management labor and manual labor the firm employs.
An individual firm's production function can be represented simply as
\begin{align}
\label{eq:lr_micro_prod}
x = \min\left\{ \frac{\bar{\alpha}\,\labor{\AIletter}}{\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\AIletter}}, \ \frac{\labor{\manualLetter}}{\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\manualLetter}} \right\},
\end{align}
where $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\AIletter}$ and $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\manualLetter}$ are the firm's \emph{skill-adjusted time requirements} per unit of output.

Two of these terms need saying precisely.
A task's skill-adjusted time is the time it takes, scaled by the wage the job containing it commands, so that an hour counts for more when the job requires the worker performing it to hold more skill.
The scaling is what lets tasks that differ in both dimensions be added up: without it, an hour of a highly skilled worker's time and an hour of a barely skilled one's would count the same, and the sum would not be a cost.
The firm's requirement of each kind of labor is then the sum of these skill-adjusted times over the tasks that use it, so $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\AIletter}$ runs over the AI-managed tasks and $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\manualLetter}$ over the manual ones together with the hand-offs, which is how the coordination costs of Section~\ref{sec:longrun.jobdesign} enter as a requirement for manual labor.
The two labor quantities $\labor{\AIletter}$ and $\labor{\manualLetter}$ are measured in these same units, so each ratio in \eqref{eq:lr_micro_prod} is an amount of output.

The remaining term is $\bar{\alpha}$, the firm's \emph{effective} AI quality,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:lr_alphabar}
\bar{\alpha} = \frac{\sum_{b}\skillAdjustedTimeAI{b}}{\sum_{b}\skillAdjustedTimeAI{b}\,\alpha^{-d_{b}}},
\end{align}
which divides the AI management labor the workflow would need if every chain succeeded on its first attempt by what it needs once retries are counted, chain by chain.
It is a time-weighted average of the success rates of the chains the firm runs, and it enters \eqref{eq:lr_micro_prod} by inflating the AI management requirement to $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\AIletter}/\bar{\alpha}$.

Effective AI quality is where the organization of work enters the aggregate.
By \eqref{eq:lr_alphabar}, $\bar{\alpha}$ averages over the chains the committed strategy actually runs, so which chains exist, how long each one is, and how much of the firm's AI management time rides on each all feed into it.
An arrangement in which AI-able steps sit next to one another supports long chains, so a given $\alpha$ compounds over many steps at once; an arrangement in which those steps are scattered among steps the AI fails at supports only short chains, and the same $\alpha$ buys less.
Note what this does and does not do here.
Because every firm commits to the same strategy over the same workflow, the arrangement of work fixes the level of effective AI quality the whole economy is organized around, and generates no variation across firms at all.
The dispersion the aggregation runs on is dispersion in what firms realize against that common level, not in how they have arranged their work.

Appendix~\ref{sec:aggregation} makes this precise.
It derives, in closed form, the distribution of $\bar{\alpha}$ across firms under which the economy admits the CES representation
\begin{align}
\label{eq:lr_macro_ces}
X = \left(\theta_{\AIletter}\,\aggregateAIlabor^{\rho} + \theta_{\manualLetter}\,\aggregateManualLabor^{\rho} + (1-\theta_{\AIletter}-\theta_{\manualLetter})\,K^{\rho}\right)^{\frac{1}{\rho}},
\end{align}
over economy-wide AI management labor $\aggregateAIlabor$, manual labor $\aggregateManualLabor$, and capital $K$.
Firms whose effective AI quality is too low to justify the cost of managing AI stay fully manual, so the aggregates in \eqref{eq:lr_macro_ces} run over the firms above an endogenous adoption threshold, and an improvement in $\alpha$ moves both that threshold and the input requirements of the firms already above it.\footnote{It is worth being clear about the scope of the two results.
The collapse in \eqref{eq:lr_micro_prod} holds for any firm and any workflow, regardless of the AI strategy $\T$ and job design $\J$, since it needs only that the technology is Leontief.
The CES in \eqref{eq:lr_macro_ces} requires more: capital productivity is common, every firm organizes identically by choosing the same AI strategy $\T$ and job design $\J$, and realized effective AI quality is the single dimension along which firms differ.}

Two things follow for how the rest of the paper should be read.
The first is permission.
The CES production functions used to study AI and labor demand at the macro level are consistent with production being sequential and with the boundaries of tasks and jobs being chosen rather than given, so nothing in our framework asks anyone to abandon that apparatus.
The second is a warning about what the apparatus hides.
Chaining and fragmentation act on $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\AIletter}$, $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\manualLetter}$, and $\bar{\alpha}$, and these enter \eqref{eq:lr_macro_ces} only through its input aggregates and weights.
Two economies whose steps are identically exposed to AI, differing only in how those steps are arranged within workflows, therefore arrive at the same functional form with different coefficients while looking alike on any step-level exposure measure.
Aggregation keeps the arrangement of work but stores it in the parameters instead of displaying it, which is why the fragmentation index has to be measured where the arrangement is still visible, at the level of the workflow.

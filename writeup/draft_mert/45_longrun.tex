So far we assumed a single worker executes the entire workflow, and that AI could improve only the time that took to execute steps. 
The results of Section~\ref{sec:results} thus followed from only that one margin.
The more consequential effects of AI, however, arrive when it does more than make a worker faster at the steps they have been given.
Once a firm is also able to change which workflow steps go to which worker, AI reshapes how work is apportioned across workers rather than only the pace at which each person's share is executed.
That is inherently a longer-run object: the firm decides jointly where to deploy AI along the workflow and where to divide it between workers, and the two decisions cannot be separated, since automating a run of steps changes what the worker who owns them has to be able to do.

This section develops that problem and closes the model in two steps.
First, we let the firm apportion the workflow across several workers, which requires two ingredients the short-run problem did not: endogenizing workers' wage, and what it costs the firm to split work among many workers.
Second, we show that individual firms' optimization problems can be represented by a single macro production function across the economy, showing that a workflow-level technology with endogenous task boundaries is consistent with the aggregate production function macroeconomists write down.
We keep both at the level of the economic content here; the full characterizations are developed in Appendices~\ref{app:jobdesign} and~\ref{sec:aggregation}.


\subsection{Job Design and the Limits of Specialization}
\label{sec:longrun.jobdesign}

Rather than give the whole workflow to one worker, the firm may divide it and hire several.
We call the contiguous block of tasks assigned to a single worker a \emph{job}, and a partition $\J = (J_1, \dotsc, J_p)$ of the task sequence into jobs a \emph{job design}.
The firm hires one worker per job, and the short-run problem of Section~\ref{sec:jobs_wages} is the special case of a single job spanning the whole workflow.

What a job pays now has to be pinned down, since the firm is choosing the job, and doing so requires one addition to the model's primitives.
We further assume that performing a step demands not only time but also a specific body of knowledge, which we call the \emph{skill} the step requires.
This extends Definitions~\ref{def:manual_task} and~\ref{def:augmented_task} to carry a skill cost alongside the time cost each already had: executing step $i$ manually now costs $(\manualSkill{i},\, \manualTime{i})$, and managing one AI attempt at it costs $(\AIskill{i},\, \AItime{i})$.
Nothing requires the two to be equal, and in general they are not, since doing the work oneself calls for knowing how to do it, whereas directing an AI to do it and judging what comes back may call for less knowledge, or for knowledge of a different kind.
A task inherits the skill of whichever mode executes it, so a task $T_b$ carries skill $\skillcost{b}$, equal to $\manualSkill{i}$ if it is a manual step and to $\AIskill{r}$ if it is an AI chain augmented at step $r$.
An automated step inside a chain demands no skill of anyone, since no human ever handles it.

Wages follow from these requirements.
Workers are ex-ante identical, so a worker assigned a job must first acquire every skill that job demands, an investment they will make only if the wage they are paid recovers it.
Competition for workers therefore ties what a job pays to the total skill it requires,\footnote{
We express skill levels directly in units of the wage they command, which is what lets the requirement and the payment be written as the same object.
Rather than formalize the underlying human capital problem here, we impose \eqref{eq:wage} as an assumption and develop a more detailed formulation in Appendix~\ref{sec:aggregation}.
}
\begin{align}
\label{eq:wage}
\text{Wage}_j = \sum_{T_b \in J_j} \skillcost{b},
\end{align}
and the firm pays this rate for as long as the job takes, regardless of which task the worker is performing at any given moment.\footnote{
This implicitly assumes a common base wage rate, so that all wage differences are driven by the human capital cost of acquiring skills.
More generally, manual and AI-assisted tasks could carry different base wage rates $w_{\manualLetter}$ and $w_{\AIletter}$, reflecting the supply of and demand for each type of labor; Equation~\eqref{eq:wage} normalizes these rates to $1$ and folds them into the skill costs $\skillcost{b}$.
We maintain this normalization for notational simplicity but it can be relaxed to more general cases.
\label{foot:wage}
}
Equation~\eqref{eq:wage} is the object the short run held fixed and normalized to $1$, and it is what the firm now chooses: a narrower job demands fewer skills and so pays less.

Left there, the firm would specialize without limit.
Splitting the workflow into ever-narrower jobs strictly lowers the wage bill, so the cost-minimizing design would assign a separate worker to every task.
What stops this is coordination.
When a worker's job ends and the work passes to the next worker, that transfer takes time: we write $\handofftime{i}$ for the hand-off time incurred when step $s_i$ is the last step of a job, and $\handofftime{}(J_j)$ for the hand-off time of job $J_j$, with $\handofftime{m} = 0$ at the end of the workflow.
Because the worker is paid for as long as the job takes, hand-off time enters the wage bill exactly as execution time does,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:lr_wagebill}
\text{WageBill}_j = \Biggl(\sum_{T_b \in J_j} \skillcost{b} \Biggr) \Biggl(\handofftime{}(J_j) + \sum_{T_b \in J_j} \timecost{b} \Biggr).
\end{align}

Equation~\eqref{eq:lr_wagebill} contains the whole trade-off.
Bundling more tasks into one job raises the first factor, since the worker must be skilled enough for all of them and is paid that rate throughout, including while performing the tasks that demand far less.
Splitting them raises the second factor, since every additional boundary inserts another hand-off.
Figure~\ref{fig:lr_job_design} makes this geometric: a job's wage bill is the area of a rectangle whose height is the skill it requires and whose width is the time it takes, and the firm is choosing how to cover the workflow with such rectangles.

In the long run the firm chooses the AI strategy and the job design together,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:lr_totalcost}
\min_{\T} \ \min_{\J} \
\sum_{J_j \in \J} \Biggl[\Biggl(\sum_{T_b \in J_j} \skillcost{b} \Biggr) \Biggl(\handofftime{}(J_j) + \sum_{T_b \in J_j} \timecost{b} \Biggr)\Biggr],
\end{align}
which nests the fixed-boundary problem \eqref{eq:totalcost_mediumrun} of Appendix~\ref{app:mediumrun_dp} as the case of a single job spanning the whole workflow.
Appendix~\ref{app:jobdesign.longrun} shows that this joint problem remains solvable to any desired accuracy.

The economics of putting the two margins together is that AI acts on job design through the skill dimension, not only the time dimension.
A chain replaces a run of steps with a single verification at its endpoint, so it strips out the skills the automated steps used to demand and leaves the worker holding only the skill needed to manage and check the chain.
That shrinks the height of the rectangle a bundled job must cover, which lowers the wage penalty from bundling and makes wider jobs cheaper to sustain: the same coordination cost now buys more.
Automation and consolidation of jobs are therefore complements in our framework, which is the sense in which AI redefines work rather than merely displacing it from a fixed set of jobs.
Working in the opposite direction, we treat hand-off costs as fixed, whereas AI plausibly lowers them too, by absorbing the peripheral coordination work of a transfer such as summarizing prior work or drafting the documentation that accompanies it.
Were that channel active, it would push toward narrower jobs and more specialization, so the net effect of AI on job breadth is an empirical question our framework poses rather than settles.\footnote{
A useful reading of the hand-off cost is that it has two components: peripheral coordination work that AI can facilitate, and a remainder that rests on tacit, interpersonal, or social knowledge and is not easily executable by AI \citep{deming2017growing}.
The hand-off cost in the model should be read as the second, irreducibly human component.
}
Appendix~\ref{app:jobdesign} develops the full problem, including a worked example in which hand-off costs overturn full specialization, and Appendix~\ref{sec:model_discussion} relates the resulting trade-off to the division of labor literature.


\subsection{From the Firm to the Macroeconomy}
\label{sec:longrun.aggregation}

Our production technology is Leontief over the steps of a workflow, with the boundaries of tasks and jobs chosen by the firm.
This is a more detailed object than the aggregate production functions used to study automation at the macro level, and it is natural to ask whether the two are compatible: does a workflow-level technology with endogenous task boundaries aggregate to anything a macroeconomist would recognize?
It does, in two steps, and the answer tells us where the firm's organizational choices show up once the economy is viewed in aggregate.

The first step collapses the workflow.
For a given AI strategy and job design, every task is executed either by AI management labor or by manual labor, and the many-input Leontief function over individual tasks reduces to a Leontief function over just those two aggregates,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:lr_micro_prod}
x = \min\left\{ \frac{\bar{\alpha}\,\labor{\AIletter}}{\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\AIletter}}, \ \frac{\labor{\manualLetter}}{\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\manualLetter}} \right\},
\end{align}
where $\labor{\AIletter}$ and $\labor{\manualLetter}$ are the firm's AI management and manual labor, and $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\AIletter}$ and $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\manualLetter}$ are the skill-adjusted time requirements of the two groups of tasks, with the hand-off times of Section~\ref{sec:longrun.jobdesign} entering $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\manualLetter}$.
The organizational problem of the previous sections survives aggregation entirely inside these coefficients: which steps are chained, where jobs are cut, and how much verification the workflow requires all enter through $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\AIletter}$ and $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\manualLetter}$, and through the firm's \emph{effective} AI quality
\begin{align}
\label{eq:lr_alphabar}
\bar{\alpha} = \frac{\sum_{b}\skillAdjustedTimeAI{b}}{\sum_{b}\skillAdjustedTimeAI{b}\,\alpha^{-d_{b}}},
\end{align}
a time-weighted harmonic average of the success probabilities of the chains the firm actually runs.
Two firms facing the same general-purpose technology $\alpha$ therefore have different $\bar{\alpha}$ when they deploy it over different arrangements of work, which is the sense in which effective AI quality is an organizational object and not only a technological one.

The second step aggregates across firms.
Individual Leontief technologies do not aggregate to a smooth production function on their own; what smooths them is heterogeneity, in the tradition of \citet{houthakker1955pareto} and \citet{levhari1968note}.
Here the natural dimension of heterogeneity is the one the model already delivers: firms differ in how effectively they put the same AI technology to work, that is, in $\bar{\alpha}$.
Appendix~\ref{sec:aggregation} shows that there is a distribution of $\bar{\alpha}$ across firms under which the economy admits the CES representation
\begin{align}
\label{eq:lr_macro_ces}
X = \left(\theta_{\AIletter}\,\aggregateAIlabor^{\rho} + \theta_{\manualLetter}\,\aggregateManualLabor^{\rho} + (1-\theta_{\AIletter}-\theta_{\manualLetter})\,K^{\rho}\right)^{\frac{1}{\rho}},
\end{align}
over economy-wide AI management labor $\aggregateAIlabor$, manual labor $\aggregateManualLabor$, and capital $K$, and derives that distribution in closed form.
Firms whose effective AI quality is too low to cover the cost of managing AI stay fully manual, so the aggregates in \eqref{eq:lr_macro_ces} are taken over the firms above an endogenous adoption threshold, and improvements in $\alpha$ move both the threshold and the input coefficients of the firms already above it.

Two things follow that matter for how our results should be read.
The first is a licence: the CES production functions used to study AI and labor demand at the macro level are consistent with production being sequential and with task boundaries being chosen rather than given, so nothing in our framework requires abandoning that apparatus.
The second is a caution about what the apparatus conceals.
The chaining results of Section~\ref{sec:comparative_advantage} and the fragmentation result of Section~\ref{sec:fragmentation} operate on $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\AIletter}$, $\skillAdjustedTimeLetter_{\manualLetter}$, and $\bar{\alpha}$, which appear in \eqref{eq:lr_macro_ces} only through the input aggregates and the CES weights.
Two economies with identical AI exposure at the step level, differing only in how those steps are arranged within workflows, therefore reach the aggregate production function with different coefficients while looking identical in any step-level exposure measure.
Aggregation preserves the arrangement of work, but it stores it in the parameters rather than displaying it, which is precisely why the fragmentation index has to be measured at the level of the workflow.

In this section, we empirically test three predictions of our model: (1) AI-executed steps tend to appear next to each other in the production sequence, forming AI chains; (2) occupations in which AI-suitable steps are more dispersed have fewer AI-executed steps; and (3) the AI-execution status of a step's neighbors affects the likelihood that the step itself is executed by AI.

For our analysis, we construct a dataset that records three attributes of each step: its AI exposure status (exposed or unexposed to AI), which we interpret as the difficulty of the step for AI, its realized mode of execution (manual, AI-augmented, or AI-automated), and its position in the production sequence.\footnote{
Our main analysis uses the full set of O*NET tasks in each occupation; the restriction to more frequently-executed subsets is discussed below and carried out in Appendix~\ref{app:frequency_robustness}.
}
To create this dataset, we combine data from four sources:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The O*NET 27.3 Database \citep{onet27_3},
    \item Human-generated labels for AI exposure of O*NET tasks from \cite{eloundou2023gpts},
    \item Realized AI execution mode of O*NET tasks from the Anthropic Economic Index \citep{handa2025economictasksperformedai},
    \item A GPT-generated task ordering for each O*NET occupation.
\end{enumerate}
The details of how the dataset is constructed are given in Appendix~\ref{app:data_prep}.

The structure of our theoretical framework closely aligns with the structure of the O*NET dataset.
Each production step in the model corresponds to an O*NET task, and each job corresponds to an O*NET occupation.
In the absence of AI, a step (that is, an O*NET task) is executed manually and maps directly into a task under the model's definition.
Thus, without AI, an O*NET task can be viewed as both a step and a task, which matches the standard treatment of tasks in the O*NET dataset.
Later when we discuss AI execution, however, multiple steps, corresponding to a subset of O*NET tasks, may be chained together and executed jointly by AI, forming a composite task per the model's definition.
Throughout this section, we use the terms step, O*NET task, and task interchangeably and expect the reader to keep these distinctions in mind.
We similarly use the terms job and occupation interchangeably without restating the mapping each time.

One feature of the data shapes how we use it throughout.
The model's automated versus augmented distinction is defined by production sequencing, whereas the Anthropic labels are assigned on task-level feasibility and are agnostic to workflow position, so the data occasionally shows an automated task followed by a manual one, which the model's definition of a chain does not allow.
We therefore do not distinguish the two labels in what follows and treat both as indicating AI execution.
Appendix~\ref{app:data_prep} documents the discrepancy and gives examples.


\subsection{Prediction \#1: Tendency to Have Runs of Consecutive AI-executed Tasks}
\label{sec:chainLength_prediction}

The first prediction of the model that we test is that, to leverage cost savings from forming longer AI chains, AI steps tend to appear in contiguous blocks rather than being scattered in the workflow.
This prediction follows from our definition of AI chains (Definition~\ref{def:ai_chain}), which requires every automated step to be followed by another automated or augmented step, and from the fragmentation argument, which suggests that for a given level of AI quality longer AI chains are more likely to emerge when AI-easy steps are clustered together in the workflow (see Examples~\ref{ex:fragmentation_interleaved} and~\ref{ex:fragmentation_clustered} in Subsection~\ref{sec:fragmentation}).

To test this, we compute the observed average AI chain length in the data and benchmark it against two randomization placebos.
First, we permute task positions within each occupation's workflow.
Second, we reassign tasks, each carrying its AI-execution label, randomly across occupations within the same major SOC group, preserving each occupation's task count and randomizing task positions within each occupation.\footnote{
We confine the reassignment to major occupation groups rather than the full dataset because tasks remain meaningful within their broad field but not across fields: reassigning a manufacturing task to a software occupation makes little sense, whereas reassigning tasks between two software occupations does.
If anything, restricting the reshuffle to major-group boundaries is the more conservative choice: each major group retains its AI intensity under the within-group reassignment, whereas randomizing tasks across the full dataset would scatter AI-executed tasks over all occupations and push the placebo average chain length even lower.}
The two placebos isolate different margins: the first holds each occupation's task composition fixed and randomizes the ordering of its workflow, while the second holds each occupation's size and each major group's AI intensity fixed and randomizes which tasks, and hence which execution labels, an occupation contains.
The observed mean AI chain length is 1.45.

These findings also mitigate two potential concerns regarding the structure of our dataset.
Were the GPT-generated workflow sequences arbitrary, or the Anthropic execution labels distributed randomly in the dataset across occupations, the observed average AI chain length would be a typical draw from the corresponding placebo distribution.
In both cases the true value instead lies at the extreme tail.
This implies that (1) the GPT-generated workflow sequences are unlikely to be chosen arbitrarily, and (2) the clustering of AI-executed tasks in the Anthropic data is not driven by chance co-occurrence of execution labels, but instead reflects systematic patterns within occupations.
We emphasize that these tests rule out either input being pure noise.
In particular, they do \emph{not} establish that the GPT task orderings we use are the ``right'' ones.

\subsection{Prediction \#2: Dispersion of AI-able Tasks Affects AI Execution Outcomes}
\label{sec:fragmentation_prediction}

The second prediction of the model that we test is that the extent of realized AI execution in an occupation is determined not just by the AI exposure status of its steps, but also by how clustered or dispersed those AI-able steps are in the workflow.
To examine this, we ask whether occupations with similar shares of their tasks exposed to AI exhibit different shares of realized AI-executed tasks depending on how dispersed their AI-able tasks are in the production sequence.
Notably, this level of dispersion is related to the fragmentation index from Section~\ref{sec:fragmentation}; we will make use of a slightly modified metric (which we call the \emph{empirical fragmentation index}, defined in more detail below) that can be calculated from our dataset.
Specifically, we estimate the following regression:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
    \text{ai\_execution}_{o} = {} & \beta_0 + \beta_1 \, \text{ai\_exposure}_{o} + \beta_2 \, \text{empirical\_fragmentation\_index}_{o} \\
    & + \beta_3 \, \text{num\_ai\_able\_steps}_{o} + \varepsilon_o,
\end{aligned}
    \label{eq:fragmentation_index_regression}
\end{equation}
where \emph{$\text{ai\_execution}_{o}$} is the share of tasks executed by AI in occupation $o$, \emph{$\text{ai\_exposure}_{o}$} is the share of AI-exposed tasks, and \emph{$\text{empirical\_fragmentation\_index}_{o}$} measures how dispersed the AI-able tasks are across the occupation's workflow. We additionally control for \emph{$\text{num\_ai\_able\_steps}_{o}$}, the number of AI-exposed (E1- or E2-labeled) steps in the occupation. Because the fragmentation index is the number of AI blocks per step, it varies mechanically with how many AI-able steps a workflow contains; conditioning on that count ensures $\beta_2$ is identified from how the AI-able steps are arranged rather than from how many of them there are.

The model predicts that occupations with higher AI exposure should have a higher share of steps executed by AI ($\beta_1 > 0$).
Conditional on exposure, it also predicts that when AI-able steps are more dispersed in the workflow, the share of realized AI-executed steps should be lower ($\beta_2 < 0$).
The intuition is that, for a given level of AI quality (parameter $\alpha$ in the model), AI chains are more likely to form when AI-easy steps appear next to each other, as discussed in Examples~\ref{ex:fragmentation_interleaved} and~\ref{ex:fragmentation_clustered} of Subsection~\ref{sec:fragmentation}.
When such clustering occurs, nearby steps that would otherwise remain manual may instead be automated as part of a chain.

To construct an empirical measure of fragmentation, we abstract from heterogeneity in tasks' underlying cost profiles, which are unobserved in our data, and focus solely on their positioning within the workflow.
We define an occupation's \emph{empirical fragmentation index} (EFI) as follows.
Assuming all AI-exposed steps turn into AI-executed tasks, the empirical fragmentation index is the ratio of the number of \emph{potential} tasks in the sense of our theoretical framework to the total number of steps, which is fixed and is measured by the raw count of O*NET tasks in that occupation.
The following example clarifies the definition.
Consider an occupation with five O*NET tasks.
If none of the five tasks are exposed to AI, then the number of tasks in the theoretical sense is five, and the EFI equals $5/5 = 1$.
If instead two consecutive O*NET tasks are exposed to AI (and thus can form an AI chain when all AI-exposed tasks turn into AI-executed tasks), the number of tasks in the model falls to four and the EFI becomes $4/5 = 0.8$.
Thus, the empirical fragmentation index decreases as more AI-exposed steps can potentially be consolidated into longer AI chains.

For the calculation of EFI, we treat all AI-exposed tasks as identical for the purpose of potential chaining and assume that all of them could, in principle, be executed by AI.
We count as AI-exposed both E1- and E2-exposed tasks, which together account for 44\% of the tasks in the dataset.\footnote{We use E1- and E2-exposed tasks as restricting attention to E1-exposed tasks alone (14\% of tasks) would leave potential AI chains too sparse for fragmentation to be meaningfully measured.}
We refer to this exposure-based measure as EFI Definition~1.

We estimate regression~\eqref{eq:fragmentation_index_regression} using this empirical fragmentation measure.

When AI-able steps are more clustered, realized AI execution is higher and occurs through contiguous chains rather than as isolated step substitutions.




\subsection{Prediction \#3: Adjacency to AI Tasks Increases Likelihood of AI Execution}
\label{sec:DWA_prediction}

The final prediction of the model that we test is that when the same step appears in two occupations, in one sitting between strong AI performers and in the other located between manual ones, it is more likely to be executed by AI in the first occupation.
Intuitively, when a step is surrounded by AI-executed steps, there are local gains from grouping it with its neighbors as part of a long AI chain that are absent when the step is situated next to manual steps.

Testing this prediction requires identifying steps that appear across different occupations.
Because O*NET tasks are occupation-specific, no single task is shared across occupations in the data.
To work around this, we rely on O*NET's broader categories of Detailed Work Activities (DWAs), which group conceptually similar tasks across occupations.
In our main sample, we drop tasks that are mapped to multiple DWAs.
We further restrict attention to DWAs that contain tasks appearing in more than one occupation.
Together, these restrictions reduce the sample to 10{,}708 tasks spread across 1{,}748 DWAs.
We treat the remaining tasks as instances of the same step appearing across different occupations and estimate the following regression\footnote{In a robustness exercise we refine our definition of ``similar'' tasks.
The resulting estimates closely mirror the patterns observed in the full sample in both sign and magnitude.
See Appendix~\ref{app:additional_robustness_pred3} for details.}:
\begin{equation}
    \Pr\!\left( \text{is\_ai}_{t} = 1 \mid X_t \right)
    =
    \Lambda\!\Big(
        \beta_0
        + \beta_1 \, \text{prev2\_is\_ai}_{t}
        + \beta_2 \, \text{prev\_is\_ai}_{t}
        + \beta_3 \, \text{next\_is\_ai}_{t}
        + \beta_4 \, \text{next2\_is\_ai}_{t}
        + \varepsilon_t
    \Big),
    \label{eq:DWA_regression_ai}
\end{equation}
where $\Lambda$ is the logistic CDF and \emph{$\text{is\_ai}_{t}$} indicates whether step $t$ is executed by AI.
The variables \emph{$\text{prev2\_is\_ai}_{t}$} and \emph{$\text{prev\_is\_ai}_{t}$} equal 1 if the step two positions before or immediately before step $t$ is AI-executed, respectively, and \emph{$\text{next\_is\_ai}_{t}$} and \emph{$\text{next2\_is\_ai}_{t}$} are defined analogously for the steps immediately after and two positions after step $t$.
In the implementation we also control for the step $t$'s AI exposure status and number of steps in the occupation it appears in.

Because we have no external benchmark for what counts as a large effect here, the magnitudes are hard to read on their own.
Appendix~\ref{app:additional_robustness_pred3} therefore re-estimates \eqref{eq:DWA_regression_ai} on 1,000 placebo datasets that randomize task positions within occupations, for each of the four specifications.
In every one, the observed immediate-neighbor effects lie to the right of the placebo distribution while the distant-neighbor effects lie to the left, so proximity does the work that the reshuffled orderings cannot reproduce by chance.

Overall, the results indicate that AI execution of immediate neighbors of the focal task increases the likelihood that the task is executed by AI, whereas execution status of more distant neighbors matters much less, especially after controlling for a range of relevant factors.
This aligns with the relatively short average AI chain length observed in the data (mean length 1.45), implying that tasks two positions away rarely fall in the same chain as the focal task.
It is possible that with improvements in AI quality, chains become longer and execution status of more distant neighbors becomes more relevant for the execution mode of the focal task.


\subsection{Robustness Tests}
\label{sec:robustness}

Each of the three tests above is defined over adjacent steps, so all of them rest on the task ordering rather than on task content alone.
Two features of how we obtain that ordering warrant scrutiny, and we address each with a separate exercise.

The first concern is the prompt.
A single prompt produces a single set of sequences, so if the phrasing of our request is what organizes the tasks, the resulting ordering might be a property of our instructions rather than of the work itself.
In Appendix~\ref{app:gptPrompts_robustness} we re-generate the O*NET task sequences under ten alternative prompts that vary the framing and the ordering logic, and re-run all three tests on each of the resulting datasets.
The estimates move within a narrow band, and none of the conclusions reported above turns on which prompt is used.

The second concern is the O*NET occupation task list itself.
O*NET catalogues all the work activities an occupation involves, including tasks that are rarely performed, and such tasks might act as ``filler'' steps that lengthen a workflow and separate steps that are adjacent in practice.
In Appendix~\ref{app:frequency_robustness} we restrict the sample to progressively more frequently-executed tasks, which makes the surviving sequences more workflow-like, and recover the same patterns for all three predictions.
Confidence intervals widen as the restriction shrinks the sample, since dropping infrequent tasks also removes AI-executed ones, which are already scarce to begin with, but the direction of the headline results remains unaffected.

Both exercises above stay within the bounds of the O*NET dataset and our own imputation, so neither can establish that the imputed order corresponds to how work steps are actually sequenced.
To externally validate our sequencing approach, in Appendix~\ref{app:external_validation} we turn to a second production taxonomy: the Process Classification Framework (PCF) of the American Productivity \& Quality Center (APQC), whose member working groups have recorded the activities and tasks composing a firm's business processes in the order practitioners perform them \citep{apqc_pcf}.
We first shuffle the steps of documented PCF branches and ask the LLM to order them, under our main sequencing prompt and each of the ten alternatives.
The LLM recovers the practitioners' order to a decent extent, placing $70\%$ of consecutive step pairs in the documented direction and reproducing $24\%$ of branches exactly, with an average Kendall's $\tau$ of $0.53$ against PCF's orderings that barely moves across prompts.\footnote{
Kendall's $\tau$ is a standard rank correlation measure that, roughly speaking, captures the fraction of task pairs that appear in the same relative order across two ranked lists.
This measure ranges from $-1$ (completely reversed orderings) to $1$ (identical orderings), with $0$ indicating no systematic overlap.  
Higher values therefore correspond to greater similarity between task sequences.
}

Having established that the LLM can recover a documented ordering, we then match O*NET tasks to PCF activities using natural language processing techniques to carry the AI exposure and AI execution labels over to a corpus that no LLM ordered, and re-test our predictions there.
Although this matching procedure is lossy and not all AI-exposed or AI-executed tasks are matched to an activity in the PCF corpus, Predictions~\#1 and~\#2 come through with essentially the same results: AI-executed steps co-occur far more than the same labels arranged at random along the same sequences, and the empirical fragmentation index coefficient stays negative and significant at a magnitude comparable to the main sample.
We are unable to re-run Prediction~\#3, however, whose sample requirements are considerably more restrictive, since it needs the same step to recur across workflows with differing execution outcomes and the O*NET-PCF matched data does not retain enough of that variation.

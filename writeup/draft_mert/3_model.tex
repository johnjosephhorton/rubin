A firm's production consists of a sequence of steps $\mathcal{S}=(s_1,\ldots,s_m)$.\footnote{
We hold the set of production steps fixed and abstract from the creation or disappearance of steps in our model.
}
Each step can be performed manually, performed with AI assistance (augmentation), or, when chained with neighboring steps, executed by AI with no direct human involvement (automation).
Completing a step takes worker time, and how much it takes differs across the modes of execution.
The firm groups contiguous subsequences of steps into \emph{tasks}, choosing how to deploy AI across the workflow to minimize the time production takes.
Figure~\ref{fig:steps_tasks} illustrates this procedure.

\subsection{Steps}
\label{sec:model.steps}

Steps are the smallest unit of work in our framework.
An individual step can be completed either with AI or manually (without AI).
If the firm assigns step $i$ to manual execution (denoted by $\manualLetter$), a human worker spends time $\manualTime{i}$ completing the step without AI.
Alternatively, if the firm assigns step $i$ to AI-assisted execution (denoted by $\AIletter$), a worker spends time $\AItime{i}$ completing the step in collaboration with AI.\footnote{
This could, for example, represent the time spent formulating a prompt for AI and checking the response, or ``managing'' the AI.
}
Such a collaboration succeeds independently with some (step-dependent) probability $q_i=\alpha^{d_i}$, where $\alpha$ is the quality of the general purpose AI technology and $d_i$ represents how ``difficult'' step $i$ is for AI.
The collaboration must be repeated until it succeeds, incurring the time cost of $\AItime{i}$ per iteration for a total expected time cost of $\AItime{i}/q_i$.

Definitions~\ref{def:manual_task} and~\ref{def:augmented_task} formalize the two modes of performing a single step.

\begin{definition}[Manual Step]
\label{def:manual_task}
A step $i$ is performed manually if it is executed entirely by a human worker without AI assistance.
The time cost of a manual step is denoted by $\manualTime{i}$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[Augmented Step]
\label{def:augmented_task}
A step $i$ is augmented if it is executed by the AI, after which its output is reviewed and approved by a human worker.
The expected time cost of managing a single augmented step is $\AItime{i}/q_i$, where $q_i = \alpha^{d_i} \in (0,1]$ is the AI's probability of successfully completing the step, increasing in the quality $\alpha$ of the general-purpose AI technology and decreasing in the step's difficulty $d_i$ for AI.
\end{definition}



\subsection{Tasks}
\label{sec:model.tasks}

Tasks are the smallest unit of work for a human worker.
A step performed on its own, whether manually or with AI augmentation, constitutes a task, with an expected time cost equal to that of the step in its chosen mode: $\manualTime{i}$ if performed manually and $\AItime{i}/q_i$ if augmented.

Steps can also be executed in an \emph{automated} mode in which AI performs the step end-to-end with no direct human involvement, and hence at zero direct human cost.\footnote{That execution of automated steps do not incur any cost to the firm is consistent with the AI subscription business model, where the firm pays an upfront fixed cost to an AI provider and uses AI tools at zero marginal cost afterward. The mechanisms discussed in the model remain unaffected even if we assume each time the firm deploys AI on a step it has to incur a marginal cost, but we abstract away from considering this to avoid complicating the model.}
Automation, however, arises only within an \emph{AI chain}: a contiguous run of AI-executed steps in which each step's output feeds directly into the next and a human verifies only the final output.
Every step of such a chain but the last is automated while the last step is augmented, since its output is the one the worker reviews.
The AI chain as a whole can be thought as a single \emph{composite} task delegated by the human to AI for execution.

Consider an AI chain spanning steps $(s_\ell, \dotsc, s_r)$.
The worker manages the chain by prompting for and evaluating only its final step $s_r$, since all earlier steps run automatically ``under the hood'' and remain beneath the worker's awareness.
Managing one attempt at the chain therefore costs the same as augmenting step $s_r$, namely time $\AItime{r}$ per attempt.
An attempt succeeds only if AI completes every step of the chain, which, assuming independent failures, occurs with probability $\prod_{i=\ell}^{r} q_i$; the chain is retried until it succeeds, for a total expected time of $\AItime{r}/\prod_{i=\ell}^{r} q_i$.
The chain's expected time cost is therefore $\AItime{r}/\prod_{i=\ell}^{r} q_i$.
Since $q_i = \alpha^{d_i}$, the chain succeeds with probability $\alpha^{\sum_{i=\ell}^{r} d_i}$, so $\sum_{i=\ell}^{r} d_i$ is the chain's total difficulty, aggregating additively over its constituent steps.
Finally, an AI chain may consist of a single step, with $\ell = r$: having no automated predecessors, it is simply an augmented step.
An AI-augmented step is thus an AI chain of length one, a convention we adopt throughout the paper.

Definitions~\ref{def:automated_task} and~\ref{def:ai_chain} formalize the automated execution mode and the AI chain.

\begin{definition}[Automated Step]
\label{def:automated_task}
A step is automated if it is executed entirely by AI without direct human intervention, and its output is passed directly to a subsequent augmented or automated step.
The direct human time cost of an automated step is zero.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}[AI Chain]
\label{def:ai_chain}
An AI chain is a contiguous block of one or more sequential steps executed by AI, in which all steps but the final one are automated and the final step is augmented.
An AI chain spanning steps $(s_\ell,\dots,s_r)$ has steps $(s_\ell,\dots,s_{r-1})$ automated and step $s_r$ augmented, with expected time cost $\AItime{r}/\prod_{i=\ell}^{r} q_i$.
\end{definition}

Finally, we define an \emph{AI deployment strategy} (or \emph{AI strategy} for short) as a partition of the step sequence $\mathcal{S}$ into a sequence of contiguous tasks $\mathcal{T}$, each of which is either a manual step or an AI chain.
The strategy in Figure~\ref{fig:steps_tasks}, for instance, groups seven steps into five tasks: two AI chains (a length-three chain containing Steps 2--4 and a length-one chain containing only Step~6) and three manual steps.

\subsection{Production}
\label{sec:jobs_wages}

The firm employs human workers to perform tasks and produce the final output.
Labor is the only input used in production.\footnote{
Viewed as a production function, our sequential technology is Leontief at the level of the individual firm: producing a unit of output requires completing every step of the workflow, in fixed proportions.
If different firms deploy and operationalize the AI technology with different effectiveness, however, the economy can be represented by a single production function at the macro level. In Appendix~\ref{sec:aggregation} we show that under the appropriate form of cross-firm AI deployment heterogeneity the economy can be aggregated across firms and represented by a CES production function over aggregate AI management labor, manual labor, and capital.
}
Each worker is assigned a contiguous group of tasks in the production sequence, which we call a \emph{job}.
The firm can in principle split its workflow into several jobs and hire a separate worker for each, choosing where one job ends and the next begins.
That choice is what reallocates work across workers, and it is the subject of Section~\ref{sec:longrun}.
Throughout this section and the next we hold the boundary of a job fixed and exogenous, and, without loss, treat the entire step sequence $\mathcal{S} = (s_1, \dotsc, s_m)$, which the AI strategy organizes into the task sequence $\T$, as a single worker's job.

With the job fixed, a worker is in place and is paid for the entire time the job takes to complete.
The rate they are paid does not depend on how AI is deployed, since it is settled by the job the firm has already hired them for, so the firm's cost varies only with how long the job runs.
The duration of a job is the total expected time requirement of its tasks,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:job_duration}
\sum_{T_b \in \T} \timecost{b},
\end{align}
and this is the object AI can move.
Section~\ref{sec:longrun} takes up what determines the rate itself, which becomes the relevant margin once the firm is free to redraw the job.




\subsection{Firm's Cost Minimization Problem}
\label{sec:model.jobs}

The firm deploys AI across its workflow to minimize production costs.
Labor is the only cost of production, and with the job fixed the firm's cost is the rate its worker is paid times the duration \eqref{eq:job_duration} of the job.
Because that rate is settled and is owed for as long as the job runs no matter how AI is deployed, the firm takes it as given, and cost minimization reduces to compressing the time the workflow takes.
The firm therefore chooses its AI deployment strategy $\T$ to solve
\begin{align}
\label{eq:totalcost_shortterm}
\min_{\T} \ \sum_{T_b \in \T} \timecost{b}.
\end{align}
AI is deployed on a step, or on a run of steps as a chain, whenever doing so lowers this expected time.

Problem \eqref{eq:totalcost_shortterm} is where chaining does its work, and it is the problem the rest of the paper analyzes.
Note that the firm cannot solve it step by step.
Whether automating a step lowers expected time depends on the arrangement the step sits in, since a step costs nothing directly once it is absorbed into a chain but adds its failure risk to every attempt at that chain, so the firm must choose among whole arrangements rather than making a separate decision at each step.
The number of such arrangements grows exponentially in the number of steps $m$.

\paragraph{Interpretation and solution.}
The problem above describes how a firm adapts to AI in the short run, when the boundary of the job is fixed.
Having already hired a worker for a designated block of the workflow, the firm cannot reallocate steps across workers or change what the job pays, so its only lever is where in the workflow to deploy AI, and it pulls that lever to compress the time the workflow takes.
Taking the wage as given is what lets us set it aside and work with expected time alone, which is why the model to this point needs no notion of skill at all: time savings are the whole of the short-run problem.
Section~\ref{sec:longrun} takes up the longer horizon, in which AI changes not only how fast a worker gets through their assigned steps but which steps are assigned to them, and it is there that what a job pays becomes a margin the firm chooses; Table~\ref{tab:short_long_run} summarizes how the horizons differ.
Despite the exponential number of arrangements, \eqref{eq:totalcost_shortterm} is exactly solvable in polynomial time by dynamic programming, walking the workflow from left to right and at each step either performing it manually or closing an AI chain that ends there, optimizing over where that chain began.
We defer the characterization to Appendix~\ref{app:shortrun_dp}.
Nothing in what follows depends on how the optimum is computed, and we work with the cost-minimizing arrangement directly from here on.

In this section we draw out three implications of AI chaining in the model.
First, we show that chaining can overturn comparative advantage logic: a step that is cheaper to perform manually in isolation may nonetheless be automated in the optimal configuration of production (Section~\ref{sec:comparative_advantage}).
Second, we introduce a \emph{fragmentation index} that captures how dispersed a workflow's AI-suitable steps are, and show that it approximates the cost of the firm's optimal AI strategy, so that the gains from AI depend on the arrangement of AI-able steps, not merely on what fraction of a workflow's steps are suitable for AI execution (Section~\ref{sec:fragmentation}).
Third, we show that the marginal gains from improving AI quality are non-monotone, abruptly rising whenever improvements in AI quality make redesigning workflows worthwhile (Section~\ref{sec:nonlinear}).
We collect the proofs of all formal results in this section in Appendix~\ref{app:omitted_proofs}.

\subsection{Overturning of Comparative Advantage}
\label{sec:comparative_advantage}

For a single step, the decision whether or not to deploy AI depends simply on whether the manual execution cost exceeds the AI augmentation cost.
With more than one step, however, the optimal deployment of AI depends on more than each step's own characteristics, because of the benefits of automating multiple steps together in an AI chain.
As a result, the factor of production holding the comparative advantage on a step \emph{in isolation} need not be the one that performs it in the optimal production configuration.
That is, a step that is more cost-effective to perform manually may nonetheless be automated as part of a chain.

To see what determines the execution mode of a step, consider a human-advantaged step $k$ whose successor $k+1$ is executed as a standalone augmented step, and compare two ways of handling the pair while leaving the rest of the workflow untouched: chaining steps $k$ and $k+1$ together, or performing step $k$ manually alongside the augmented step $k+1$.\footnote{The augmented step $k+1$ can be thought of as a longer AI chain terminating augmented at some step $r > k+1$. Such a chain can be considered as a single augmented step with composite cost parameters, namely verification cost $\AItime{r}$ and success probability $\prod_{i=k+1}^{r} q_i$, so \eqref{eq:ca_test} applies unchanged with these composite values.}
Chaining is the cheaper arrangement exactly when
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ca_test}
\underbrace{\frac{\AItime{k+1}}{q_k\, q_{k+1}}}_{\text{chain over $\{k, k+1\}$}}
\;<\;
\underbrace{\manualTime{k} + \frac{\AItime{k+1}}{q_{k+1}}}_{\text{$k$ manual, $k+1$ augmented}}
\quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad
\underbrace{\frac{\AItime{k+1}\,(1-q_k)}{q_k\, q_{k+1}}}_{\text{marginal cost of folding step $k$ in}}
\;<\;
\manualTime{k}.
\end{equation}
Chaining automates step $k$ at zero direct cost: the worker still verifies only the same endpoint $k+1$, and, importantly, since an automated step's output is never verified on its own, step $k$'s own verification cost $\AItime{k}$ never enters.
The only additional expense is that the chain now also fails whenever AI fails at step $k$.
Subtracting the augmentation cost of step $k+1$ from both sides of the first inequality in \eqref{eq:ca_test} makes this explicit.
Chaining pays exactly when the endpoint's verification cost, inflated by step $k$'s failure risk $1-q_k$, falls below the manual cost it displaces.
What decides automation at the margin is thus not the step's standalone comparison of manual and augmentation costs, but the comparison of its optimal standalone execution cost with its marginal contribution to an adjacent chain, a quantity that shrinks toward zero as AI executes the step more reliably ($q_k \to 1$).

Chaining forward with an attractive successor is not the only route into automation, however.
Even when condition \eqref{eq:ca_test} fails, so that on its own merits step $k$ would remain manual, the human-advantaged step can still end up automated when an AI-friendly step precedes it.
To see why, introduce a predecessor step at position $k-1$ that AI executes very reliably (i.e., high $q_{k-1}$) but that is expensive to carry out on its own, whether manually or as a standalone augmented step.
Because a chain pays only for the verification of its augmented endpoint per attempt, such a step is cheapest to absorb at the start of a chain, where its own direct cost goes unpaid; and because chains are contiguous, the chain it anchors must run forward through step $k$ to reach the cheap verification endpoint at $k+1$.
Folding the pair $\{k-1, k\}$ into the chain costs the endpoint's verification inflated by the pair's joint failure risk $1 - q_{k-1}q_k$. 
Decomposing this risk as $1 - q_{k-1}q_k = (1-q_{k-1}) + q_{k-1}(1-q_k)$ splits the marginal cost into a share for each folded step,
\[
\underbrace{\frac{\AItime{k+1}\,(1-q_{k-1}q_k)}{q_{k-1}\,q_k\,q_{k+1}}}_{\text{marginal cost of folding the pair $\{k-1, k\}$ in}}
\;=\;
\underbrace{\frac{\AItime{k+1}\,(1-q_{k-1})}{q_{k-1}\,q_k\,q_{k+1}}}_{\text{step $k-1$'s share}}
\;+\;
\underbrace{\frac{\AItime{k+1}\,(1-q_k)}{q_k\,q_{k+1}}}_{\text{step $k$'s share}}.
\]
Notice that step $k$'s share is exactly the marginal cost in \eqref{eq:ca_test}, which by supposition exceeds the manual cost it replaces: step $k$ does not pay its own way into the chain.
The predecessor's share, by contrast, is governed by its failure risk $1 - q_{k-1}$ alone, so it shrinks toward zero as AI executes the step more reliably ($q_{k-1} \to 1$), even while the standalone execution cost it displaces stays large.
Whenever the predecessor's cost saving exceeds step $k$'s shortfall, running $\{k-1, k, k+1\}$ as a single AI chain augmented at $k+1$ is strictly cheaper than executing each step on its own based on comparative advantage. 
The human-advantaged step will thus be pulled under AI by its predecessor's characteristics, not its own.

Proposition~\ref{prop:ca_local} formalizes this and makes a more general claim.

\begin{proposition}[Overturning of Comparative Advantage]
\label{prop:ca_local}
Hold the execution of the workflow outside the block $\{k-1,\, k,\, k+1\}$ fixed, and let no chain cross the block's boundary.
Let the human hold the comparative advantage on step $k$, so that $\manualTime{k} < \AItime{k}/q_k$.
Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] Whatever step $k$'s own parameters, there is a nonempty open set of neighbor parameters at which the cost-minimizing arrangement has AI execute step $k$ no matter how wide the human's advantage on it is.
\item[(ii)] Fix the cost parameters of the three steps and let their success probabilities vary. If AI executes step $k$ at $(q_{k-1},\, q_k,\, q_{k+1})$, then it does so at every $(q'_{k-1},\, q'_k,\, q'_{k+1})$ with $q'_i \geq q_i$ for each $i$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Intuitively, part~(i) of Proposition~\ref{prop:ca_local} says that whether a step is executed by AI depends not just on its own cost parameters, as comparative advantage logic would predict, but also on those of its neighbors.
A step with identical characteristics can thus be automated in one workflow where it sits beside an AI-suitable run, yet remain manual in another where AI completes the neighboring steps only with low probability.
This is precisely the variation we exploit in our empirical design to show that the AI execution likelihood of a step depends on its local work context in different occupations.
Where part~(i) says that being human-advantaged carries no guarantee of being executed one way or the other, part~(ii) says that whatever the answer at a given set of parameters, making a step and/or its neighbors friendlier to AI can only push it toward AI execution.
A step that AI executes thus never reverts to manual as its neighbors become more reliable, and the same holds when the step's own reliability improves alongside theirs.

Nonetheless, the forces determining the execution mode of a single step in the global optimum are the ones outlined here.



\subsection{Workflow-level AI Exposure and the Fragmentation Index}
\label{sec:fragmentation}

Whether, and how much, a workflow benefits from AI depends not only on how many of its steps AI can reliably perform, but on where those AI-reliable steps sit relative to one another.
The reason is a local complementarity that chaining creates for AI execution: a chain is verified once however many steps it spans, so a run of AI-reliable steps placed side by side can share a single verification, whereas the same steps separated by other steps that AI is likely to fail must each carry a verification of their own.
Steps that are not worth assigning to AI individually, on their own merits, can therefore be worth assigning to it together, once the verifications they would separately have required collapse into one and that saving outweighs the additional failure risk a longer chain carries.
This is what makes the firm's short-run problem \eqref{eq:totalcost_shortterm} more than a sequence of step-by-step decisions, and it is why the gain a workflow realizes from a given stock of AI-able steps is a property of how those steps are arranged rather than of how many of them there are.
The two examples below make this point using workflows built from four identical steps placed in two different orders.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:fragmentation_interleaved}
Consider a workflow containing four steps built from the AI-hard step of Example~\ref{ex:ca_predecessor} and a version of its AI-easy step that AI completes less reliably:
\[
\text{\emph{AI-hard} step:}\;\; \bigl(\manualTime{i},\, \AItime{i},\, q_i\bigr) = (6,\,3.5,\,0.3),
\qquad
\text{\emph{AI-easy} step:}\;\; \bigl(\manualTime{i},\, \AItime{i},\, q_i\bigr) = (8,\,6,\,0.7).
\]
The workflow alternates them, so that steps $1$ and $3$ are AI-easy and steps $2$ and $4$ are AI-hard.

Note that the two workflows contain exactly the same steps and differ only in the order in which those steps are performed, yet one of them uses no AI while the other hands half of its steps to AI.
Dispersion of AI-friendly steps can therefore stop AI exposure from converting into AI execution altogether, rather than merely making that execution dearer, and it is this margin that we explore empirically later.

To formalize this, return to the firm's AI deployment problem in \eqref{eq:totalcost_shortterm} and consider the special case in which AI verification costs are normalized, $\AItime{i} = 1$ for all $i$, so that every step takes the same time to prompt and verify.
Suppose each step $s_i$ succeeds independently with probability $q_i$, and say that any step that does not succeed \emph{fails}.
Write $F$ for the set of steps that fail and $\mathcal{C}$ for the random collection of maximal connected components of non-failed steps, and assign each $C \in \mathcal{C}$ the weight $\omega(C) = \min\bigl\{ 1, \sum_{s_i \in C} \manualTime{i} \bigr\}$, which is $1$ unless the manual costs of its steps sum to less than the normalized cost of verifying one AI attempt.
The realized fragmentation of the workflow is then
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{s_i \in F}\min\left\{\manualTime{i}, \frac{1}{q_i}\right\} + \sum_{C \in \mathcal{C}} \omega(C),\label{eq:fragmentation}
\end{equation}
and the \emph{fragmentation index} of the workflow is its expected value.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:fragmentation}
   Fix a sequence of $m$ steps $\mathcal{S} = (s_1, \dotsc, s_m)$, each with normalized AI verification cost $\AItime{i} = 1$.
   Let $FI$ denote its fragmentation index and let $OPT$ denote the minimum cost over all AI strategies.
   Then $\tfrac{1}{8}OPT \leq FI \leq \tfrac{5}{4}OPT$.
   If we further assume $\manualTime{i} \geq 1$ for all $i$, then $\tfrac{1}{4}OPT \leq FI \leq \tfrac{5}{4}OPT$.
\end{proposition}

Proposition~\ref{prop:fragmentation} says that a single closed-form quantity tracks, up to constant factors, what the firm's whole optimization is worth, so a workflow with a high fragmentation index yields less benefit from AI however cleverly the firm deploys it.
What \eqref{eq:fragmentation} measures is easiest to see through a prophet who can look ahead and tell which steps AI would complete on its first attempt.
Runs of steps that will all succeed are natural candidates for a chain, so the prophet chains each such run and executes every remaining step on its own, in whichever of the two modes is cheaper for it; the fragmentation index is the expected cost of that strategy.
This is not the optimal strategy even with foresight, since a long chain with a good chance of success may be worth attempting even when the prophet knows it will fail on the first try.
What Proposition~\ref{prop:fragmentation} establishes is that its cost nonetheless approximates the cost of the optimal strategy, which enjoys no foresight at all.

Read this way, the index is lower exactly when the steps AI performs reliably are clustered, since a long run of such steps is more likely to survive as a single connected component once failures are realized, and a single component is charged only once.
The driver of efficiency gains from AI automation is therefore not simply the expected number of steps AI can perform effectively, but the number of \emph{adjacent} such steps in the workflow.
A further implication of the proof is a natural and approximately optimal greedy algorithm for constructing AI strategies: the firm groups steps into a chain as long as the probability of success stays sufficiently high, terminating the chain and starting a new one when it falls too low, and converting chains of length one to manual execution where that is cheaper.



\subsection{Non-Monotonicity of Gains from AI Improvements}
\label{sec:nonlinear}

In its short-run cost minimization problem \eqref{eq:totalcost_shortterm}, the firm optimizes costs over a discrete set of feasible AI strategies.
Here we show that the gains from marginal improvements in AI quality accrue non-monotonically, because such improvements eventually trigger discrete reorganizations of work.
Recall that $\alpha$ measures the quality of the general-purpose AI technology, so that advances in that technology are represented by increases in its value.

When AI quality is so low that the firm deploys AI nowhere, marginal improvements offer no return at all: every step is performed manually, and production costs do not depend on $\alpha$.
Once $\alpha$ passes the level at which deploying AI somewhere in the workflow becomes worthwhile, further improvements do pay off, but at a diminishing rate, since a more reliable AI is being applied to a fixed set of AI-executed steps.
And when $\alpha$ reaches a level at which the firm re-optimizes its deployment strategy, extending a chain to absorb steps that were previously manual, the marginal return jumps up discontinuously, because improvements in quality now compound over a longer stretch of the workflow.
We call the values of $\alpha$ at which the optimal strategy changes \emph{reorganization thresholds}: between them the returns to AI progress diminish, and at each of them those returns jump upward as longer AI chains become worth deploying.

Seeing what this implies requires one ingredient the model leaves out.
We do not price improvements in AI quality, but a firm plainly pays for them, whether by investing in the technology or by renting a more capable model, and that is how the exercise should be read.
On that reading the first stretch of $\alpha$ is one over which the firm spends and gets nothing back, and the returns begin only once quality crosses the first reorganization threshold, which is the shape \citet{brynjolfsson2021productivity} describe as the productivity J-curve.
The example below traces these thresholds in a simple two-step workflow.

We formalize the non-monotonicity in marginal returns to improving AI quality in Appendix~\ref{app:nonmonotone}.
There we show that the cost of any fixed AI strategy varies smoothly with $\alpha$, so that the firm's optimal cost is the lower envelope of finitely many smooth cost curves, and prove in Lemma~\ref{lem:reorg_threshold} that the marginal gains from improving AI diminish monotonically within each regime where a single strategy remains optimal and jump upward at every reorganization threshold.

The reorganizations these thresholds trigger are, so far, confined to the short run. 
The firm rearranges which steps AI executes while the boundaries of jobs and wages stay fixed.
That confinement is what puts the result in perspective.
The return to a general-purpose technology depends less on how capable the technology is at a moment in time than on how much of production has been rearranged to exploit it, and the rearrangement available in the short run is the narrowest kind there is.
Historical examples such as the steam engine, electricity, and the computer each delivered only modest improvements in the early days of their adoption, and their full impact arrived once production had been reorganized around them.
Arguably, improvements in AI quality take place over a horizon long enough for firms to not only use it on their current workflows but also reorganize them so that they can gain from the full capabilities of AI, so the thresholds studied here are the visible edge of a larger reorganization, and it is that larger one we study next.

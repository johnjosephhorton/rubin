In this section we draw out three implications of AI chaining in the model.
First, we show that chaining can overturn comparative advantage logic: a step that is cheaper to perform manually in isolation may nonetheless be automated in the optimal configuration of production (Section~\ref{sec:comparative_advantage}).
Second, we introduce a \emph{fragmentation index} that captures how dispersed a workflow's AI-suitable steps are, and show that it approximates the cost of the firm's optimal AI strategy, so that the gains from AI depend on the arrangement of AI-able steps, not merely on what fraction of a workflow's steps are suitable for AI execution (Section~\ref{sec:fragmentation}).
Third, we show that the marginal gains from improving AI quality are non-monotone, abruptly rising whenever improvements in AI quality make redesigning workflows worthwhile (Section~\ref{sec:nonlinear}).
We collect the proofs of all formal results in this section in Appendix~\ref{app:omitted_proofs}.

\subsection{Overturning of Comparative Advantage}
\label{sec:comparative_advantage}

For a single step, the decision whether to deploy AI compares the cost of manual execution with the cost of AI augmentation.
With more than one step this comparison is no longer decisive, because a step can be folded into an AI chain that spans its neighbors.
The factor of production holding the comparative advantage on a step \emph{in isolation} therefore need not be the one that performs it in the optimal configuration of production: a step that is cheaper to perform manually may nonetheless be executed by AI.

To see what determines the execution mode of a step, consider an \emph{AI-hard} step $k$, that is, one on which the human holds the comparative advantage, whose successor $k+1$ is \emph{AI-easy} and executed as a standalone augmented step, and compare two ways of handling the pair while leaving the rest of the workflow untouched: chaining steps $k$ and $k+1$ together, or performing step $k$ manually alongside the augmented step $k+1$.\footnote{The augmented step $k+1$ can be thought of a longer AI chain terminating augmented at some step $r > k+1$. Such a chain can be considered as a single augmented step with composite cost parameters, namely management cost $\AItime{r}$ and success probability $\prod_{i=k+1}^{r} q_i$, so \eqref{eq:ca_test} applies unchanged with these composite values.}
Chaining is the cheaper arrangement exactly when
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ca_test}
\underbrace{\frac{\AItime{k+1}}{q_k\, q_{k+1}}}_{\text{chain over $\{k, k+1\}$}}
\;<\;
\underbrace{\manualTime{k} + \frac{\AItime{k+1}}{q_{k+1}}}_{\text{$k$ manual, $k+1$ augmented}}
\quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad
\underbrace{\frac{\AItime{k+1}\,(1-q_k)}{q_k\, q_{k+1}}}_{\text{marginal cost of folding step $k$ in}}
\;<\;
\manualTime{k}.
\end{equation}
Chaining automates step $k$ at zero direct cost: the worker prompts for and verifies only the endpoint $k+1$, so step $k$'s own AI management cost $\AItime{k}$ never enters, and the sole additional expense is that the chain now also fails whenever AI fails at step $k$.
The second inequality in \eqref{eq:ca_test} isolates that expense: chaining pays exactly when the endpoint's management cost, inflated by step $k$'s failure risk $1-q_k$, falls below the manual cost it displaces.
What decides automation at the margin is thus not the step's standalone comparison of manual and augmentation costs, but its marginal contribution to an adjacent chain, which shrinks toward zero as AI executes the step more reliably ($q_k \to 1$).

An attractive successor downstream is not the only route into automation.
Even when condition \eqref{eq:ca_test} fails, so that on its own merits step $k$ would remain manual, an AI-friendly \emph{predecessor} can still pull it under AI.
Because a chain pays only for the management of its augmented endpoint per each attempt, a step that AI executes very reliably but that is expensive to carry out on its own, whether manually or as a standalone augmented step, is cheapest to absorb at the start of a chain, where its own direct cost goes unpaid; and because chains are contiguous, the chain such a step $k-1$ anchors must run forward through step $k$ to reach the cheap verification endpoint at $k+1$.
Folding the pair $\{k-1, k\}$ in costs the endpoint's management inflated by the pair's joint failure risk $1 - q_{k-1}q_k$, which decomposes as $(1-q_{k-1}) + q_{k-1}(1-q_k)$ and splits the marginal cost into a share for each folded step,
\[
\underbrace{\frac{\AItime{k+1}\,(1-q_{k-1}q_k)}{q_{k-1}\,q_k\,q_{k+1}}}_{\text{marginal cost of folding the pair $\{k-1, k\}$ in}}
\;=\;
\underbrace{\frac{\AItime{k+1}\,(1-q_{k-1})}{q_{k-1}\,q_k\,q_{k+1}}}_{\text{step $k-1$'s share}}
\;+\;
\underbrace{\frac{\AItime{k+1}\,(1-q_k)}{q_k\,q_{k+1}}}_{\text{step $k$'s share}}.
\]
Step $k$'s share is exactly the marginal cost in \eqref{eq:ca_test}, which by supposition exceeds the manual cost it replaces, so step $k$ does not pay its own way into the chain.
The predecessor's share, by contrast, is governed by its failure risk $1 - q_{k-1}$ alone, so it shrinks toward zero as $q_{k-1} \to 1$ even while the standalone execution cost it displaces stays large.
Whenever that saving exceeds step $k$'s shortfall, chaining all three steps beats executing each as comparative advantage dictates, and step $k$ is pulled under AI by its predecessor's characteristics rather than its own.
Example~\ref{ex:ca_predecessor} showcases these forces numerically.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:ca_predecessor}

Step~1 is \emph{hard} for AI and Step~2 is \emph{easy}; the same two steps reappear in Examples~\ref{ex:fragmentation_interleaved} and~\ref{ex:fragmentation_clustered} below.

In isolation the two steps fall on opposite sides: the human holds the comparative advantage on Step~1 by a wide margin, $\manualTime{1} = 6$ against an augmentation cost of $\AItime{1}/q_1 \approx 11.67$, while AI holds it on Step~2, where augmentation costs $\AItime{2}/q_2 \approx 4.44$ against $\manualTime{2} = 8$.
Taking the two steps on their own (the top row of the figure), each is executed the way comparative advantage dictates, at a total of $10.44$; chaining them would cost $\AItime{2}/(q_1 q_2) \approx 14.81$, since the chain inherits Step~1's failure risk.

Now introduce the predecessor, Step~0 (the bottom row of the figure).
Step~0 is reliable for AI ($q_0 = 0.95$) but expensive to execute on its own in either mode, costing $25$ manually and $\AItime{0}/q_0 \approx 26.32$ augmented, so the only cheap way to absorb it is to place it at the start of a chain, where its management cost goes unpaid.
The cost-minimizing strategy therefore chains all three steps, augmented at Step~2, at cost $\AItime{2}/(q_0\, q_1\, q_2) \approx 15.60$, below the $35.44$ of the arrangement comparative advantage would dictate and below every other strategy, the cheapest of which chains Steps~0--1 and augments Step~2 at $16.73$.
Step~1, on which the human holds close to a two-fold advantage in isolation, is thus automated only to bridge the AI-friendly predecessor to the cheap endpoint at Step~2, and since its own success probability is just $q_1 = 0.3$ the chain frequently fails and is retried; the firm accepts this because handling Step~0 in any other way is more costly still.

The example in fact contains both routes by which a step can be automated.
Chaining Steps~1--2, priced at $14.81$ and rejected, would have automated Step~1 from downstream, while adding Step~0 automates it from upstream at $15.60$.
Proposition~\ref{prop:ca_local} records what the firm's choice among the available arrangements does and does not depend on.

\begin{proposition}[Local Overturning of Comparative Advantage]
\label{prop:ca_local}
Let the human hold the comparative advantage on step $k$, so that $\manualTime{k} < \AItime{k}/q_k$, hold the execution of the workflow outside the block $\{k-1,\, k,\, k+1\}$ fixed, and let no chain cross the block's boundary.
Then:
\begin{enumerate}
\item[(i)] Whatever step $k$'s own parameters, and however wide the human's advantage on it, there is a nonempty open set of neighbor parameters at which the cost-minimizing arrangement has AI execute step $k$, and the same holds separately for each chain through which AI can reach the step. Comparative advantage on a step therefore places no restriction on the mode in which that step is executed.
\item[(ii)] Fix step $k$'s own parameters. If AI executes step $k$ at neighbor success probabilities $(q_{k-1},\, q_{k+1})$, then it does so at every $(q'_{k-1},\, q'_{k+1})$ with $q'_{k-1} \geq q_{k-1}$ and $q'_{k+1} \geq q_{k+1}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{proposition}

Throughout, write $\minTime{j} = \min\left\{\manualTime{j}, \frac{\AItime{j}}{q_j}\right\}$ for the cheapest way of executing step $j$ on its own, whether manually or as a standalone augmented step, and take $q_{k-1}, q_k, q_{k+1} \in (0,1)$.
Because chains are contiguous and none crosses the boundary of the block $\{k-1,\, k,\, k+1\}$, an arrangement of the block is a partition of it into consecutive runs, each run executed either as a single step at cost $\minTime{j}$ or as an AI chain augmented at its last step $r$, at cost $\AItime{r}/\prod_i q_i$ taken over the run's steps.

Before comparing them, note which of these arrangements can have AI execute step $k$ at all.
Executed as a singleton, step $k$ costs $\minTime{k} = \manualTime{k}$, since $\manualTime{k} < \AItime{k}/q_k$ by hypothesis, so augmenting it on its own is strictly dominated by performing it manually and never occurs in an optimum.
AI therefore executes step $k$ only if step $k$ belongs to a chain of length at least two, and since chains are contiguous and confined to the block, that chain is $\{k,\, k+1\}$, $\{k-1,\, k,\, k+1\}$, or $\{k-1,\, k\}$: in the first two, step $k$ is not the chain's last step, so it is never prompted or verified and is automated, while in the third it is the last step, hence augmented.

Pricing a singleton $\{k\}$ at $\manualTime{k}$ accordingly, the four partitions cost
\begin{align*}
V_0 &= \minTime{k-1} + \manualTime{k} + \minTime{k+1}, && \text{(no chain covers step $k$)} \\
V_1 &= \minTime{k-1} + \frac{\AItime{k+1}}{q_k\, q_{k+1}}, && \text{(chain $\{k,\, k+1\}$)} \\
V_2 &= \frac{\AItime{k+1}}{q_{k-1}\, q_k\, q_{k+1}}, && \text{(chain $\{k-1,\, k,\, k+1\}$)} \\
V_3 &= \frac{\AItime{k}}{q_{k-1}\, q_k} + \minTime{k+1}. && \text{(chain $\{k-1,\, k\}$)}
\end{align*}
AI executes step $k$ exactly when $\min\{V_1, V_2, V_3\} < V_0$, ties being resolved in favor of manual execution.

\begin{proof}[Proof of part (i)]
Fix $(\manualTime{k}, \AItime{k}, q_k)$ with $\manualTime{k} < \AItime{k}/q_k$ and fix $q_{k-1}, q_{k+1} \in (0,1)$.
Restrict attention to the two-parameter family of neighbor costs given by $\manualTime{k-1} = \AItime{k-1} = \mu > 0$ and $\manualTime{k+1} = 1$, $\AItime{k+1} = \tau > 0$, so that $\minTime{k-1} = \mu$ and $\minTime{k+1} = \min\{1, \tau/q_{k+1}\}$.
Since $V_0, \dotsc, V_3$ are continuous in all of the neighbor parameters, it suffices to exhibit, for each configuration, values of $(\mu, \tau)$ at which its cost is strictly below the other three; strictness then extends the conclusion to an open neighborhood.

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Configuration $\{k,\, k+1\}$, which automates step $k$.}
Set $\tau = q_k\, q_{k+1} \manualTime{k}/2$, so that $V_1 = \mu + \manualTime{k}/2$, and take
\[ 0 < \mu < \min\left\{ \frac{\manualTime{k}}{2}\cdot\frac{1-q_{k-1}}{q_{k-1}},\ \frac{\AItime{k}}{q_{k-1}\, q_k} - \frac{\manualTime{k}}{2} \right\}, \]
a nonempty interval because $q_{k-1} < 1$ and $\AItime{k}/(q_{k-1} q_k) > \AItime{k}/q_k > \manualTime{k}$.
The three comparisons are then
\begin{align*}
V_0 - V_1 &= \frac{\manualTime{k}}{2} + \minTime{k+1}, \\
V_2 - V_1 &= \frac{\manualTime{k}}{2}\cdot\frac{1-q_{k-1}}{q_{k-1}} - \mu, \\
V_3 - V_1 &= \frac{\AItime{k}}{q_{k-1}\, q_k} - \frac{\manualTime{k}}{2} - \mu + \minTime{k+1},
\end{align*}
and all three are positive: the first because $\minTime{k+1} > 0$, and the second and third by the first and second bounds on $\mu$ respectively.

\item \emph{Configuration $\{k-1,\, k,\, k+1\}$, which also automates step $k$.}
Set $\mu = 1$ and take $0 < \tau < q_{k+1} \min\{q_{k-1}\, q_k,\ \AItime{k}\}$, so that the long chain is cheap.
The three comparisons are
\begin{align*}
V_0 - V_2 &= 1 + \manualTime{k} + \minTime{k+1} - \frac{\tau}{q_{k-1}\, q_k\, q_{k+1}}, \\
V_1 - V_2 &= 1 + \frac{\tau}{q_k\, q_{k+1}} - \frac{\tau}{q_{k-1}\, q_k\, q_{k+1}}, \\
V_3 - V_2 &= \frac{\AItime{k}}{q_{k-1}\, q_k} + \minTime{k+1} - \frac{\tau}{q_{k-1}\, q_k\, q_{k+1}}.
\end{align*}
The bound on $\tau$ holds $\tau/(q_{k-1} q_k q_{k+1})$ below both $1$ and $\AItime{k}/(q_{k-1} q_k)$, which leaves all three positive.

\item \emph{Configuration $\{k-1,\, k\}$, which augments step $k$.}
Set $\mu = \AItime{k}/(q_{k-1} q_k)$ and take $\tau > \max\{q_{k+1},\ q_k q_{k+1},\ q_{k-1} q_k q_{k+1} (\mu+1)\}$.
The first of these bounds puts $\tau/q_{k+1}$ above $1$, so that $\minTime{k+1} = 1$ and $V_3 = \mu + 1$, and the comparisons are
\begin{align*}
V_0 - V_3 &= \manualTime{k}, \\
V_1 - V_3 &= \frac{\tau}{q_k\, q_{k+1}} - 1, \\
V_2 - V_3 &= \frac{\tau}{q_{k-1}\, q_k\, q_{k+1}} - (\mu + 1),
\end{align*}
positive because $\manualTime{k} > 0$ and by the second and third bounds on $\tau$ respectively.
\end{itemize}

The margin of the human's advantage on step $k$, $\AItime{k}/q_k - \manualTime{k}$, was unrestricted throughout, which gives the second claim of part~(i).
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}[Proof of part (ii)]
Fix step $k$'s own parameters together with the four cost parameters of its neighbors, and let the two neighbor success probabilities vary over $Q = (0,1) \times (0,1)$.
Write
\[ \mathcal{A} \;=\; \bigl\{ (q_{k-1},\, q_{k+1}) \in Q \; : \; \text{AI executes step } k \bigr\} \]
for the set of neighbor reliabilities at which the cost-minimizing arrangement does not leave step $k$ to the human.
Part~(ii) is precisely the statement that $\mathcal{A}$ is \emph{upward closed} in the coordinatewise order: whenever a pair belongs to $\mathcal{A}$, so does every pair at least as large in both coordinates.
The proof turns that statement into one about the three differences
\[ D_j \;=\; V_j - V_0, \qquad j = 1,\, 2,\, 3, \]
each of which compares one arrangement that has AI execute step $k$ against the arrangement that does not.
Three reductions get us there.

\emph{Reduction 1: from $\mathcal{A}$ to one chain at a time.}
AI executes step $k$ exactly when $\min\{V_1, V_2, V_3\} < V_0$, that is, when $D_j < 0$ for at least one $j$.
Writing $\mathcal{A}_j = \{(q_{k-1},\, q_{k+1}) \in Q : D_j < 0\}$ for the set on which chain $j$ by itself beats manual execution of step $k$, this says
\[ \mathcal{A} \;=\; \mathcal{A}_1 \cup \mathcal{A}_2 \cup \mathcal{A}_3 . \]
A union of upward-closed sets is upward closed, because a pair in the union lies in one of them and every larger pair then lies in that same one.
It is therefore enough to show that each $\mathcal{A}_j$ is upward closed, and the three can be handled separately: we never need to know which chain is optimal, only whether each one, on its own, is cheaper than leaving the step to the human.

\emph{Reduction 2: from both coordinates at once to one at a time.}
Suppose each $\mathcal{A}_j$ is upward closed in $q_{k-1}$ at every fixed value of $q_{k+1}$, and upward closed in $q_{k+1}$ at every fixed value of $q_{k-1}$.
Given $(q_{k-1},\, q_{k+1}) \in \mathcal{A}_j$ and a larger pair $(q'_{k-1},\, q'_{k+1})$, raise the two coordinates in turn:
\[ (q_{k-1},\, q_{k+1}) \in \mathcal{A}_j \;\implies\; (q'_{k-1},\, q_{k+1}) \in \mathcal{A}_j \;\implies\; (q'_{k-1},\, q'_{k+1}) \in \mathcal{A}_j , \]
the first step by closure in the first coordinate holding $q_{k+1}$ fixed, the second by closure in the second coordinate holding $q'_{k-1}$ fixed.
The one-coordinate statements deliver the joint one, provided each holds at every value of the coordinate being held fixed, which is how they are proved below.

\emph{Reduction 3: from upward closure to a property of $D_j$.}
With one coordinate fixed, $D_j$ is a function of a single success probability $q$, and $\mathcal{A}_j$ is the set on which that function is negative.
That set is upward closed as soon as $D_j$, once negative, stays negative as $q$ rises.
It suffices for $D_j$ to be nonincreasing in $q$, which is what we verify in every case but one; in the exceptional case $D_j$ increases, and we show instead that it is negative on the entire range where the increase can occur.

Writing the three differences out,
\begin{align*}
D_1 &= \frac{\AItime{k+1}}{q_k\, q_{k+1}} - \manualTime{k} - \minTime{k+1}, \\
D_2 &= \frac{\AItime{k+1}}{q_{k-1}\, q_k\, q_{k+1}} - \minTime{k-1} - \manualTime{k} - \minTime{k+1}, \\
D_3 &= \frac{\AItime{k}}{q_{k-1}\, q_k} - \minTime{k-1} - \manualTime{k},
\end{align*}
the terms that move with the neighbor reliabilities are the chain costs and the two standalone costs
\[ \minTime{k-1} = \min\left\{ \manualTime{k-1},\, \frac{\AItime{k-1}}{q_{k-1}} \right\}, \qquad \minTime{k+1} = \min\left\{ \manualTime{k+1},\, \frac{\AItime{k+1}}{q_{k+1}} \right\}, \]
of which the first depends on $q_{k-1}$ alone and the second on $q_{k+1}$ alone.

\emph{Closure in $q_{k-1}$.}
Fix $q_{k+1}$ and let $q = q_{k-1}$ vary.
The difference $D_1$ does not depend on $q$ at all.
The two arrangements it compares, the chain $\{k,\, k+1\}$ and manual execution of step $k$, both leave the predecessor to be executed on its own, so the term $\minTime{k-1}$ appears in $V_1$ and $V_0$ alike and cancels.
A constant function is nonincreasing, so $\mathcal{A}_1$ is upward closed in this coordinate.

The other two differences share a common form.
Both compare a chain that starts at the predecessor against an arrangement that executes the predecessor on its own, so both can be written
\[ D(q) \;=\; \underbrace{\frac{A}{q}}_{\text{chain starting at } k-1} \;-\; \underbrace{\min\left\{ \manualTime{k-1},\, \frac{B}{q} \right\}}_{\text{predecessor on its own}} \;-\; \underbrace{c}_{\text{fixed}}, \qquad B = \AItime{k-1}, \]
where $A$ and $c$ are strictly positive, neither depends on $q$, and
\begin{align*}
D_2: \quad A &= \frac{\AItime{k+1}}{q_k\, q_{k+1}}, & c &= \manualTime{k} + \minTime{k+1}, \\
D_3: \quad A &= \frac{\AItime{k}}{q_k}, & c &= \manualTime{k}.
\end{align*}
The predecessor's standalone cost is the smaller of a manual cost that does not move with $q$ and an augmented cost $B/q$ that falls as $q$ rises, and the two cross at
\[ q^{*} \;=\; \frac{B}{\manualTime{k-1}} \;=\; \frac{\AItime{k-1}}{\manualTime{k-1}} , \]
so the predecessor is cheapest manually for $q \leq q^{*}$ and cheapest augmented for $q > q^{*}$.
Splitting there,
\[
D(q) \;=\;
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{A}{q} - \manualTime{k-1} - c, & 0 < q \leq q^{*}, \\[2.2ex]
\dfrac{A - B}{q} - c, & q^{*} < q < 1,
\end{cases}
\]
the second line obtained by substituting $\minTime{k-1} = B/q$.
The two expressions agree at $q = q^{*}$, where $\manualTime{k-1} = B/q^{*}$, so $D$ is continuous; and if $q^{*} \geq 1$ the second branch is empty and only the first arises.

On the first branch $D$ is strictly decreasing, since
\[ \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}q}\left( \frac{A}{q} - \manualTime{k-1} - c \right) \;=\; -\frac{A}{q^{2}} \;<\; 0 . \]
Raising $q$ here cheapens the chain while leaving untouched the manual predecessor cost that the chain displaces.

On the second branch the behavior depends on the sign of $A - B$:
\begin{itemize}
\item if $A \geq B$, then $(A-B)/q$ is nonincreasing in $q$, so $D$ is nonincreasing on the branch;
\item if $A < B$, then $D$ increases with $q$, but $(A-B)/q < 0$ and $-c < 0$, so $D(q) < 0$ at \emph{every} point of the branch.
\end{itemize}
Either way, if $D$ is negative at some point of the branch it is negative everywhere to the right of it.

Now take $q < q'$ in $(0,1)$ with $D(q) < 0$, and consider the three possible positions of the pair relative to $q^{*}$:
\begin{itemize}
\item if $q' \leq q^{*}$, both lie on the first branch and $D(q') < D(q) < 0$;
\item if $q^{*} < q$, both lie on the second branch, which was just handled;
\item if $q \leq q^{*} < q'$, then $D(q^{*}) \leq D(q) < 0$ by the first branch, and since the second-branch expression agrees with $D$ at $q^{*}$ and the argument above applies to it on $[q^{*},\, 1)$, we get $D(q') < 0$.
\end{itemize}
So $\{D < 0\}$ is upward closed, and with it $\mathcal{A}_2$ and $\mathcal{A}_3$.
The economics behind the two branches is the same: raising $q_{k-1}$ always cheapens a chain that starts at the predecessor, and it cheapens the predecessor's own execution only once that execution is an augmented one, in which case both costs carry the same factor $1/q$ and fall in the same proportion, so the comparison between them cannot reverse.

\emph{Closure in $q_{k+1}$.}
Now fix $q_{k-1}$ and let $q_{k+1}$ vary.
The difference $D_3$ does not depend on $q_{k+1}$, for the mirror-image reason: the chain $\{k-1,\, k\}$ and manual execution of step $k$ both leave the successor to be executed on its own, so $\minTime{k+1}$ cancels between $V_3$ and $V_0$, and $\mathcal{A}_3$ is upward closed in this coordinate.

For $D_1$ and $D_2$, change variable to the successor's augmented cost
\[ u \;=\; \frac{\AItime{k+1}}{q_{k+1}} , \]
which is strictly decreasing in $q_{k+1}$.
Upward closure in $q_{k+1}$ is then the same as the requirement that $D$, once negative, stay negative as $u$ \emph{falls}, for which it suffices that $D$ be nondecreasing in $u$.
Every appearance of $q_{k+1}$ is through $u$: the chain costs in $V_1$ and $V_2$ are $u/q_k$ and $u/(q_{k-1} q_k)$, and the successor's standalone cost is $\minTime{k+1} = \min\{\manualTime{k+1},\, u\}$.
Both differences therefore take the form
\[ D \;=\; \lambda u \;-\; \min\{ \manualTime{k+1},\, u \} \;-\; c' , \]
where $c'$ does not move with $u$ and
\begin{align*}
D_1: \quad \lambda &= \frac{1}{q_k}, & c' &= \manualTime{k}, \\
D_2: \quad \lambda &= \frac{1}{q_{k-1}\, q_k}, & c' &= \minTime{k-1} + \manualTime{k} .
\end{align*}
In both cases $\lambda \geq 1$, since $q_{k-1}$ and $q_k$ are at most $1$.
Splitting at the point where the successor switches between augmented and manual execution,
\[
\lambda u - \min\{ \manualTime{k+1},\, u \} \;=\;
\begin{cases}
(\lambda - 1)\, u, & u \leq \manualTime{k+1}, \\[1ex]
\lambda u - \manualTime{k+1}, & u > \manualTime{k+1},
\end{cases}
\]
which is nondecreasing on each branch, with slopes $\lambda - 1 \geq 0$ and $\lambda > 0$, and continuous where the branches meet, both sides taking the value $(\lambda - 1)\manualTime{k+1}$ at $u = \manualTime{k+1}$.
Hence $D$ is nondecreasing in $u$ and so nonincreasing in $q_{k+1}$, and $\mathcal{A}_1$ and $\mathcal{A}_2$ are upward closed in that coordinate.
What $\lambda \geq 1$ says is that a chain running through the successor pays the same management cost $\AItime{k+1}$ that a standalone augmented successor pays, inflated by the failure risk of the steps ahead of it, so a cheaper endpoint helps the chain by at least as much as it helps the alternative.

Each $\mathcal{A}_j$ is therefore upward closed in each coordinate separately, hence upward closed in the coordinatewise order by Reduction~2, and $\mathcal{A}$ is upward closed by Reduction~1.
\end{proof}

Part~(i) says that the human's advantage on the step, however wide, is uninformative about the step's fate once its neighbors are allowed to vary.
Only three chains can reach step $k$, since a chain covering it must be contiguous and a human-advantaged step is never worth augmenting on its own: $\{k,\, k+1\}$ and $\{k-1,\, k,\, k+1\}$, which automate it, and $\{k-1,\, k\}$, which augments it as the chain's endpoint.

Proposition~\ref{prop:ca_local} is a \emph{local} statement, and deliberately so.
It compares rearrangements of a step and its immediate neighbors while holding the AI strategy on the rest of the workflow fixed, so it does not by itself settle whether the overturning survives in the cost-minimizing arrangement of the entire production sequence, which is a property of the whole workflow at once.
A window can lose its overturning to what lies outside it: if the steps before the window make it worthwhile to run a chain that terminates augmented at $k-1$, then step $k-1$ is spent as that chain's endpoint and can no longer anchor a chain running forward through step $k$, which is left with only the downstream comparison it fails.
A window can also be handed one, since a chain formed for reasons entirely outside it, running from before $k-1$ to past $k+1$, sweeps step $k$ into automation whether or not any rearrangement of the window would.
A global counterpart would have to quantify over the arrangements of all $m$ steps at once, and the conditions it delivered would be far more restrictive without saying more about the mechanism.
What decides execution modes in the global optimum are nonetheless the forces isolated here, weighing a step's standalone cost against its marginal contribution to whatever chain its neighbors make available.

Figure~\ref{fig:ca_regions} shows the four arrangements at work.
It holds every parameter of Example~\ref{ex:ca_predecessor} fixed except the success probabilities of Steps~$k-1$ and~$k$, and shades how the cost-minimizing arrangement executes Step~$k$ as those two vary, with the example itself marked by a star; the human keeps the comparative advantage on Step~$k$ throughout, since the horizontal axis stops where augmenting the step in isolation would break even against its manual cost of $6$.
Step~$k$ is nonetheless performed manually only in the lower left, and, as part~(ii) requires, improving AI on either the predecessor or the step itself never returns execution there: moving right forms the chain $\{k,\, k+1\}$ at $q_k \approx 0.43$, where the failure risk Step~$k$ adds to it falls below the manual work it displaces, while moving up leaves Step~$k$ untouched and reaches the three-step chain by way of the band in which the chain stops at Step~$k$.\footnote{The orange band is not a general feature. When managing AI at Step~$k$ is expensive enough relative to executing Step~$k+1$ on its own, stopping the chain at Step~$k$ never pays, and the firm crosses directly from manual execution to automation.}
The order along that vertical path is not incidental: the pair $\{k-1, k\}$ must first become reliable enough to be worth chaining at all, and only once the risk accumulated upstream is small is it worth carrying the chain on to the cheap endpoint at Step~$k+1$.

Whether a step is executed by AI thus depends not just on its own cost parameters, as comparative advantage logic would predict, but also on those of its neighbors: a step with identical characteristics can be automated in one workflow, where it sits beside an AI-suitable run, yet remain manual in another, where AI completes the neighboring steps only with low probability.
Part~(ii) of Proposition~\ref{prop:ca_local} is what turns this into something we can test.
The primitives of the model, the management costs $\AItime{i}$ and the success probabilities $q_i$, are not observed, and part~(i) warns that knowing a step's own primitives would not pin down how the step is executed even if we did observe them.
Part~(ii) supplies a restriction that survives this ignorance: whatever the unobserved parameters happen to be, an improvement in a neighbor's reliability moves the focal step's execution in one direction only.
Its content is not that the effect is positive on average but that it never reverses, which is not obvious from the cost comparisons.
Raising a neighbor's success probability cheapens the chains that would cover the focal step, but it also cheapens that neighbor's own standalone execution, so one might expect a sufficiently reliable predecessor to become worth augmenting by itself rather than anchoring a chain, or a sufficiently reliable successor to become cheap enough to verify separately, in either case leaving the focal step to be performed by hand.
Part~(ii) rules this out: when the neighbor's cheapest standalone execution is the manual one its cost does not fall at all, and when it is the augmented one the standalone cost and the chain cost fall in the same proportion, so a better neighbor can only pull the step under AI.
Neighbors' execution modes are themselves outcomes rather than primitives, but they are driven by the same success probabilities, so the model predicts a positive association between a step's AI execution and that of its neighbors, with the sign fixed in advance rather than read off the estimates.
Section~\ref{sec:DWA_prediction} tests exactly this, comparing the same step across occupations whose local work contexts differ in how much of the surrounding work AI executes.


\subsection{Workflow-level AI Exposure and the Fragmentation Index}
\label{sec:fragmentation}

Even though characterizing the execution mode of each individual step in the optimal arrangement is hard, the overall gains from deploying AI across a workflow can still be studied without knowing precisely which steps end up automated.
% Here we show that what governs these gains is not just how many of a workflow's steps are AI-friendly but how those steps are arranged relative to one another.
Returning to the firm's AI deployment problem described in \eqref{eq:totalcost_shortterm} and Section~\ref{sec:shortrun.production}, we can interpret workflow-level AI exposure as the extent to which an optimal AI strategy employs AI automation and augmentation.
Intuitively, a run of consecutive steps in the workflow that an AI can perform effectively is a natural candidate for an AI chain.
A workflow that contains such runs of consecutive steps is therefore likely to benefit most from AI automation.
On the other hand, a workflow in which AI-friendly steps are separated by intermediate steps that an AI is likely to fail is less exposed to large-scale automation, even though its step-level exposure to AI may appear high. 
The two examples below showcase this point.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:fragmentation_interleaved}
Consider a workflow of four steps that alternate between the \emph{easy} and \emph{hard} steps of Example~\ref{ex:ca_predecessor}, namely its Steps~2 and~1:
\[ \bigl(\manualTime{i},\, \AItime{i},\, q_i\bigr) = (8,\,4,\,0.9) \ \text{ for $i = 1, 3$ (easy)}, \qquad (6,\,3.5,\,0.3) \ \text{ for $i = 2, 4$ (hard)}. \]
Figure~\ref{fig:ex_dispersed} shows the arrangement and its optimal execution.

The same steps thus yield very different automation outcomes depending only on whether the AI-friendly ones are clustered or dispersed.
Note what does and does not change between the two arrangements.
The same two steps are executed by AI either way, since an isolated easy step is already worth augmenting on its own; what the arrangement changes is what that AI execution costs, because clustering lets a single verification cover two steps instead of two verifications covering one each.
Where an isolated exposed step is \emph{not} worth augmenting on its own, a case the model equally permits, dispersion suppresses AI execution outright rather than merely making it dearer, and it is that margin the AI-execution shares of Section~\ref{sec:empirics} pick up.

To make this intuition more precise, let us return to the firm's AI deployment problem in \eqref{eq:totalcost_shortterm}.
Consider the special case where AI management costs are normalized: $\AItime{i} = 1$ for all $i$.
That is, each step requires the same time to prompt and manage one attempt by the AI.
Under this assumption, we will define a measure of the dispersion of AI-exposed steps in a workflow, which we refer to as the \emph{fragmentation index} of a workflow.
We then show that the fragmentation index of a workflow approximates (up to constant factors) the cost of an optimal AI strategy for that workflow.
In other words, workflows for which the fragmentation index is high will tend to yield less benefit from AI automation.
Notably, this measure speaks to the chaining problem and abstracts from job design, which we take up in Section~\ref{sec:longrun}.

Intuitively, the fragmentation index is motivated by a hypothetical scenario where a prophet can ``see the future'' to determine which tasks would be completed successfully by AI on its first attempt and which would not.
If many tasks in sequence will all complete successfully on the first try, these are a good candidate for an AI chain.
The prophet might therefore chain together all contiguous sequences of tasks that would all succeed, and perform all other tasks manually.
The fragmentation index is then the expected cost of this strategy, under the assumption that the prophet's foresight is correct.
Notably, this AI strategy isn't necessarily optimal even with the benefit of foresight: a long AI chain with a good probability of success might be optimal even if the prophet knows that it will fail on the first attempt.
However, what we show is that its cost approximates the cost of the optimal strategy even without foresight.

To define the fragmentation index more formally, suppose that each step $s_i$ succeeds independently with probability $q_i$; any task that does not succeed is said to fail.
Write $F$ for the set of steps that fail, and $\mathcal{C} = \{C_1, \dotsc, C_k\}$ for the random variable representing the collection of maximal connected components of non-failed steps.
The weight of each $C_j \in \mathcal{C}$ is defined to be $\omega(C_j) = \min\{ 1, \sum_{i \in C_j} \manualTime{i} \}$.
That is, each $C_j$ has weight $1$ unless the sum of the manual costs of its steps is less than $1$ (due to our assumption that AI management costs are normalized to $1$).

Given a realization of $\mathcal{C}$ and $F$, we define the realized fragmentation to be
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{i \in F}\min\left\{\manualTime{i}, \frac{\AItime{i}}{q_i}\right\} + \sum_{C_j \in \mathcal{C}} \omega(C_j).\label{eq:fragmentation}
\end{equation}
The \emph{fragmentation index} is defined to be the expected value of the realized fragmentation.

Intuitively, we expect the fragmentation index to be lower when highly automatable steps (that is, those for which the AI are likely to succeed) are clustered together, since a large cluster of highly automatable steps are more likely to realize as a single connected component when failures are realized.

We will show that the fragmentation index is within a constant factor of the cost of the optimal AI strategy for a given step sequence.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:fragmentation}
   Fix a sequence of $m$ steps $\mathcal{S} = \{s_1, \dotsc, s_m\}$, each with normalized AI management cost $\AItime{i} = 1$.
   Let $FI$ denote its fragmentation index and let $OPT$ denote the minimum cost over all AI strategies.
   Then $\tfrac{1}{8}OPT \leq FI \leq \tfrac{5}{4}OPT$.
   If we further assume $\manualTime{i} \geq 1$ for all $i$, then $\tfrac{1}{4}OPT \leq FI \leq \tfrac{5}{4}OPT$.
\end{proposition}

The key take-away from Proposition~\ref{prop:fragmentation} is that workflows with high fragmentation index (i.e., those for which the expected number of \emph{consecutive} successful step executions by an AI is low) will tend to have higher costs even under optimal use of AI chaining.
A key driver of efficiency gains via AI automation is therefore not simply the expected number of steps that can be automated effectively, but the number of \emph{adjacent} steps in the workflow that have high exposure to AI.

An implication of our proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:fragmentation} is a natural and approximately optimal greedy algorithm for constructing AI strategies.
The algorithm groups tasks into chains as long as the probability of success is sufficiently high; if the success probability falls too low then the chain is terminated and a new chain is started (with chains of length 1 converted to manual execution when appropriate).



\subsection{Non-Monotonicity of Gains from AI Improvements}
\label{sec:nonlinear}

Improvements to AI technology naturally lead to improvements in the overall cost of production, but these effects need not be monotone.
The full economic impact of a disruptive general-purpose technology can take significant time and investment to materialize, with notable historical examples ranging from the steam engine to electricity to computers.
In each case, a new technology enables modest short-term improvements to existing workflows, but the full impact is not felt until large-scale reorganization of production is enabled \citep{brynjolfsson2021productivity}.
This drives a potentially non-monotone marginal value from technology improvements, where major improvements are only unlocked after surpassing a threshold of capability at which they become feasible.

Our framework captures such non-monotonicities in the marginal value of technological improvements.
This arises in our model via optimization over different AI deployment strategies.
One can think of parameter $\alpha \in (0,1]$ in our model as a measure of the quality of a general purpose AI technology, taken to be the probability that a task of normalized difficulty $1$ is completed successfully.
We can then consider general technology improvements as increasing the value of $\alpha$.
When AI quality is so low that the firm deploys AI nowhere, marginal improvements in $\alpha$ offer no return at all: every step is performed manually, and production costs do not depend on AI quality.
Once $\alpha$ passes the level at which deploying AI somewhere in the workflow becomes worthwhile, further improvements pay off, but at a diminishing rate, as a more reliable AI is being applied to a fixed set of AI-executed steps.
And when $\alpha$ reaches a level at which the firm reorganizes its deployment strategy, extending a chain to automate steps that were previously manual, the marginal return to AI quality jumps up discontinuously, because quality improvements now compound over a longer stretch of the workflow.
We refer to the values of $\alpha$ at which the optimal strategy changes as \emph{reorganization thresholds}: they partition the range of AI quality into regimes, within which the returns to AI progress diminish, while at each threshold the returns jump upward.
The example below shows this pattern at work in a two-step workflow.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:nonmonotone}
Consider the AI-hard and AI-easy steps of Example~\ref{ex:ca_predecessor} once more, now with their difficulties written out, so that the AI succeeds at step~$i$ with probability $q_i = \alpha^{d_i}$:
\[ \bigl(\manualTime{i},\, \AItime{i},\, d_i\bigr)_{i=1,2} = (6,\,3.5,\,11),\quad (8,\,4,\,1). \]
At an AI quality of about $\alpha = 0.9$, these difficulties deliver approximately the success probabilities used in those examples, $q_1 \approx 0.3$ and $q_2 = 0.9$.

The non-monotonicity in marginal returns to improving AI is a general phenomenon, and we formalize it in Appendix~\ref{app:nonmonotone}.
There we show that the cost of any fixed AI strategy varies smoothly with $\alpha$, so that the firm's optimal cost is the lower envelope of finitely many smooth cost curves, and prove in Lemma~\ref{lem:reorg_threshold} that the marginal gains from improving AI diminish monotonically within each regime where a single strategy remains optimal and jump upward at every reorganization threshold.

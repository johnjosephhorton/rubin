In this section we draw out three implications of AI chaining in the model.
First, we show that chaining can overturn comparative advantage logic: a step that is cheaper to perform manually in isolation may nonetheless be automated in the optimal configuration of production (Section~\ref{sec:comparative_advantage}).
Second, we introduce a \emph{fragmentation index} that captures how dispersed a workflow's AI-suitable steps are, and show that it approximates the cost of the firm's optimal AI strategy, so that the gains from AI depend on the arrangement of AI-able steps, not merely on what fraction of a workflow's steps are suitable for AI execution (Section~\ref{sec:fragmentation}).
Third, we show that the marginal gains from improving AI quality are non-monotone, abruptly rising whenever improvements in AI quality make redesigning workflows worthwhile (Section~\ref{sec:nonlinear}).
We collect the proofs of all formal results in this section in Appendix~\ref{app:omitted_proofs}.

\subsection{Overturning of Comparative Advantage}
\label{sec:comparative_advantage}

For a single step, the decision whether or not to deploy AI depends simply on whether the manual execution cost exceeds the AI augmentation cost.
With more than one step, however, the optimal deployment of AI depends on more than each step's own characteristics, because of the benefits of automating multiple steps together in an AI chain.
As a result, the factor of production holding the comparative advantage on a step \emph{in isolation} need not be the one that performs it in the optimal production configuration.
That is, a step that is more cost-effective to perform manually may nonetheless be automated as part of a chain.

To see what determines the execution mode of a step, consider an \emph{AI-hard} step $k$, that is, one on which the human holds the comparative advantage, whose successor $k+1$ is \emph{AI-easy} and executed as a standalone augmented step, and compare two ways of handling the pair while leaving the rest of the workflow untouched: chaining steps $k$ and $k+1$ together, or performing step $k$ manually alongside the augmented step $k+1$.\footnote{The augmented step $k+1$ can be thought of a longer AI chain terminating augmented at some step $r > k+1$. Such a chain can be considered as a single augmented step with composite cost parameters, namely management cost $\AItime{r}$ and success probability $\prod_{i=k+1}^{r} q_i$, so \eqref{eq:ca_test} applies unchanged with these composite values.}
Chaining is the cheaper arrangement exactly when
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ca_test}
\underbrace{\frac{\AItime{k+1}}{q_k\, q_{k+1}}}_{\text{chain over $\{k, k+1\}$}}
\;<\;
\underbrace{\manualTime{k} + \frac{\AItime{k+1}}{q_{k+1}}}_{\text{$k$ manual, $k+1$ augmented}}
\quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad
\underbrace{\frac{\AItime{k+1}\,(1-q_k)}{q_k\, q_{k+1}}}_{\text{marginal cost of folding step $k$ in}}
\;<\;
\manualTime{k}.
\end{equation}
Chaining automates step $k$ at zero direct cost: the worker still prompts for and verifies only the same endpoint $k+1$, and, importantly, since an automated step is never prompted or verified on its own step $k$'s own AI management cost $\AItime{k}$ never enters.
The only additional expense is that the chain now also fails whenever AI fails at step $k$.
Subtracting the augmentation cost of step $k+1$ from both sides of the first inequality in \eqref{eq:ca_test} makes this explicit.
Chaining pays exactly when the endpoint's management cost, inflated by step $k$'s failure risk $1-q_k$, falls below the manual cost it displaces.
What decides automation at the margin is thus not the step's standalone comparison of manual and augmentation costs, but the comparison of its optimal standalone execution cost with its marginal contribution to an adjacent chain, a quantity that shrinks toward zero as AI executes the step more reliably ($q_k \to 1$).

Chaining forward with an attractive successor is not the only route into automation, however.
Even when condition \eqref{eq:ca_test} fails, so that on its own merits step $k$ would remain manual, the human-advantaged step can still end up automated when an AI-friendly step precedes it.
To see why, introduce a predecessor step at position $k-1$ that AI executes very reliably (i.e., high $q_{k-1}$) but that is expensive to carry out on its own, whether manually or as a standalone augmented step.
Because a chain pays only for the management of its augmented endpoint per each attempt, such a step is cheapest to absorb at the start of a chain, where its own direct cost goes unpaid; and because chains are contiguous, the chain it anchors must run forward through step $k$ to reach the cheap verification endpoint at $k+1$.
Folding the pair $\{k-1, k\}$ into the chain costs the endpoint's management inflated by the pair's joint failure risk $1 - q_{k-1}q_k$. 
Decomposing this risk as $1 - q_{k-1}q_k = (1-q_{k-1}) + q_{k-1}(1-q_k)$ splits the marginal cost into a share for each folded step,
\[
\underbrace{\frac{\AItime{k+1}\,(1-q_{k-1}q_k)}{q_{k-1}\,q_k\,q_{k+1}}}_{\text{marginal cost of folding the pair $\{k-1, k\}$ in}}
\;=\;
\underbrace{\frac{\AItime{k+1}\,(1-q_{k-1})}{q_{k-1}\,q_k\,q_{k+1}}}_{\text{step $k-1$'s share}}
\;+\;
\underbrace{\frac{\AItime{k+1}\,(1-q_k)}{q_k\,q_{k+1}}}_{\text{step $k$'s share}}.
\]
Notice that step $k$'s share is exactly the marginal cost in \eqref{eq:ca_test}, which by supposition exceeds the manual cost it replaces: step $k$ does not pay its own way into the chain.
The predecessor's share, by contrast, is governed by its failure risk $1 - q_{k-1}$ alone, so it shrinks toward zero as AI executes the step more reliably ($q_{k-1} \to 1$), even while the standalone execution cost it displaces stays large.
Whenever the predecessor's cost saving exceeds step $k$'s shortfall, running $\{k-1, k, k+1\}$ as a single AI chain augmented at $k+1$ is strictly cheaper than executing each step on its own based on comparative advantage. 
The human-advantaged step will thus be pulled under AI by its predecessor's characteristics, not its own.

Proposition~\ref{prop:ca_local} collects these comparisons.
Because a chain is paid for through its endpoint alone, the test for any candidate chain is the same one \eqref{eq:ca_test} applies to the pair $\{k, k+1\}$: whether the endpoint's management cost, inflated by the joint failure risk of every step the chain covers, falls below what those steps would cost executed on their own.

\begin{proposition}[Local Overturning of Comparative Advantage]
\label{prop:ca_local}
Fix a step $k$ on which the human holds the comparative advantage in isolation, $\manualTime{k} < \AItime{k}/q_k$, and write $v_i = \min\{\manualTime{i},\, \AItime{i}/q_i\}$ for the cost of executing step $i$ on its own.
Hold the execution of all steps outside $\{k-1,\, k,\, k+1\}$ fixed, let no task cross the boundary of this block, and break ties in favor of manual execution.
Then AI executes step $k$ in the cost-minimizing arrangement of the block if and only if some AI chain running over steps $\ell, \dotsc, r$ with $k-1 \leq \ell \leq k \leq r \leq k+1$ and $\ell < r$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ca_local}
\frac{\AItime{r}}{\prod_{i=\ell}^{r} q_i} \;<\; \sum_{i=\ell}^{r} v_i.
\end{equation}
If in addition step $k+1$ is AI-easy, so that $v_{k+1} = \AItime{k+1}/q_{k+1}$, then \eqref{eq:ca_local} holds for some such chain if and only if at least one of
\[
\text{(i) } \ \Delta_k < \manualTime{k},
\qquad
\text{(ii) } \ \Delta_{k-1} + \Delta_k < v_{k-1} + \manualTime{k},
\qquad
\text{(iii) } \ \frac{\AItime{k}}{q_{k-1}\, q_k} < v_{k-1} + \manualTime{k},
\]
where $\Delta_k = \AItime{k+1}(1-q_k)/(q_k\, q_{k+1})$ and $\Delta_{k-1} = \AItime{k+1}(1-q_{k-1})/(q_{k-1}\, q_k\, q_{k+1})$ are the marginal costs of folding steps $k$ and $k-1$ into a chain terminating at $k+1$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Within the block $\{k-1,\, k,\, k+1\}$ a feasible arrangement is a partition of the three steps into contiguous tasks, each of which is either a manual step or an AI chain terminating in an augmented step, and the block's cost is the sum of the costs of its tasks.

We first argue that step $k$ is never augmented as a task on its own.
If it were, replacing that task with the manual execution of step $k$ would leave every other task of the block untouched and lower the cost by $\AItime{k}/q_k - \manualTime{k} > 0$.
Either the firm pays for step $k$ directly, in which case it prefers the manual mode, or it does not pay for it directly at all, which happens only when step $k$ lies inside a chain and its cost is borne through the chain's failure risk instead.
In the cost-minimizing arrangement, then, step $k$ is either executed manually on its own or it belongs to a chain covering at least one of its neighbors.

Consider the first case.
If step $k$ is manual, neither neighbor can belong to a chain: chains are contiguous and steps $k-1$ and $k+1$ are not adjacent to one another, so any chain containing either of them would have to contain step $k$.
Each neighbor is therefore a task on its own, executed at cost $v_i$, and the arrangement costs
\[
M \;=\; v_{k-1} + \manualTime{k} + v_{k+1},
\]
where $v_k = \manualTime{k}$ because $\manualTime{k} < \AItime{k}/q_k$ by assumption.

In the second case the chain covering step $k$ is one of exactly three, and the steps of the block it leaves out are again tasks on their own:
\begin{align*}
\text{(1)} \quad & \text{step $k-1$ on its own, steps $k$ and $k+1$ chained:} & v_{k-1} + \frac{\AItime{k+1}}{q_k\, q_{k+1}}, \\
\text{(2)} \quad & \text{steps $k-1$, $k$, and $k+1$ chained:} & \frac{\AItime{k+1}}{q_{k-1}\, q_k\, q_{k+1}}, \\
\text{(3)} \quad & \text{steps $k-1$ and $k$ chained, step $k+1$ on its own:} & \frac{\AItime{k}}{q_{k-1}\, q_k} + v_{k+1}.
\end{align*}
As ties are broken in favor of manual execution, AI executes step $k$ if and only if one of these three costs falls below $M$.
Each such comparison is \eqref{eq:ca_local} for the corresponding chain, since the steps the chain leaves out enter both sides at the same cost $v_i$ and cancel, which proves the first claim.

It remains to carry out the three comparisons, using $v_{k+1} = \AItime{k+1}/q_{k+1}$ where step $k+1$ is executed on its own.
In case~(1) the term $v_{k-1}$ cancels, leaving
\[
\frac{\AItime{k+1}}{q_k\, q_{k+1}} \; < \; \manualTime{k} + \frac{\AItime{k+1}}{q_{k+1}}
\qquad \Longleftrightarrow \qquad
\Delta_k = \frac{\AItime{k+1}(1-q_k)}{q_k\, q_{k+1}} \; < \; \manualTime{k},
\]
which is condition~(i).
In case~(3) the term $v_{k+1}$ cancels, leaving $\AItime{k}/(q_{k-1} q_k) < v_{k-1} + \manualTime{k}$, which is condition~(iii).
In case~(2) nothing cancels, and subtracting $\AItime{k+1}/q_{k+1}$ from both sides leaves
\[
\frac{\AItime{k+1}\,(1 - q_{k-1} q_k)}{q_{k-1}\, q_k\, q_{k+1}} \; < \; v_{k-1} + \manualTime{k}.
\]
Decomposing the chain's joint failure risk as $1 - q_{k-1}q_k = (1-q_{k-1}) + q_{k-1}(1-q_k)$ splits the left-hand side into $\Delta_{k-1} + \Delta_k$, which is condition~(ii).
\end{proof}

Condition (i) is the downstream channel of \eqref{eq:ca_test} and condition (ii) the upstream one, and in both of them step $k$ is automated.
Condition (iii) is a third route to the same outcome, in which the predecessor anchors a chain that terminates at step $k$, so that AI executes step $k$ under human supervision rather than without it.
The distinction matters for what the firm pays: step $k$'s own management cost $\AItime{k}$ appears in (iii), but in neither (i) nor (ii), since a step in the interior of a chain is never prompted or verified on its own.
The human's comparative advantage on step $k$, however wide, therefore does not enter the two conditions under which the step ends up automated.
Example~\ref{ex:ca_predecessor} showcases these forces numerically.

The second is condition~(ii), where the chain no longer stops at Step~$k$ but runs on to Step~$k+1$.

In sum, whether a step is automated depends not just on its own cost parameters (as comparative advantage logic would predict) but also on characteristics of its neighbors: the pressure to fold a human-advantaged step into a chain can come from either a cheap chain endpoint \emph{downstream} or an AI-reliable predecessor \emph{upstream}.
A step with identical characteristics can therefore be automated in one workflow, where it sits beside an AI-suitable run, yet remain manual in another, where AI completes the neighboring steps only with low probability.
This is precisely the variation we exploit in Section~\ref{sec:DWA_prediction} to show that the AI execution likelihood of a step depends on its local work context in different occupations.

We must emphasize that Proposition~\ref{prop:ca_local} and the arguments around it are \emph{local} statements: they compare rearrangements of a step and its immediate neighbors while holding the AI strategy on the rest of the workflow fixed, and so they fall short of determining whether the overturning takes place in the cost-minimizing arrangement of the entire production sequence, which depends on the whole workflow at once.
Nonetheless, the forces determining execution modes in the optimum are the ones outlined here.


\subsection{Workflow-level AI Exposure and the Fragmentation Index}
\label{sec:fragmentation}

Even though characterizing the execution mode of each individual step in the optimal arrangement is hard, the overall gains from deploying AI across a workflow can still be studied without knowing precisely which steps end up automated.
% Here we show that what governs these gains is not just how many of a workflow's steps are AI-friendly but how those steps are arranged relative to one another.
Returning to the firm's AI deployment problem described in \eqref{eq:totalcost_shortterm} and Section~\ref{sec:shortrun.production}, we can interpret workflow-level AI exposure as the extent to which an optimal AI strategy employs AI automation and augmentation.
Intuitively, a run of consecutive steps in the workflow that an AI can perform effectively is a natural candidate for an AI chain.
A workflow that contains such runs of consecutive steps is therefore likely to benefit most from AI automation.
On the other hand, a workflow in which AI-friendly steps are separated by intermediate steps that an AI is likely to fail is less exposed to large-scale automation, even though its step-level exposure to AI may appear high. 
The two examples below showcase this point.

\label{ex:fragmentation_interleaved}
Consider a workflow of four steps that alternate between the \emph{easy} and \emph{hard} steps of Example~\ref{ex:ca_predecessor}, namely its Steps~2 and~1:
\[ \bigl(\manualTime{i},\, \AItime{i},\, q_i\bigr) = (8,\,4,\,0.9) \ \text{ for $i = 1, 3$ (easy)}, \qquad (6,\,4,\,0.28) \ \text{ for $i = 2, 4$ (hard)}. \]

The same steps thus yield very different automation outcomes depending only on whether the AI-friendly ones are clustered or dispersed.
Note what does and does not change between the two arrangements.
The same two steps are executed by AI either way, since an isolated easy step is already worth augmenting on its own; what the arrangement changes is what that AI execution costs, because clustering lets a single verification cover two steps instead of two verifications covering one each.
Where an isolated exposed step is \emph{not} worth augmenting on its own, a case the model equally permits, dispersion suppresses AI execution outright rather than merely making it dearer, and it is that margin the AI-execution shares of Section~\ref{sec:empirics} pick up.

To make this intuition more precise, let us return to the firm's AI deployment problem in \eqref{eq:totalcost_shortterm}.
Consider the special case where AI management costs are normalized: $\AItime{i} = 1$ for all $i$.
That is, each step requires the same time to prompt and manage one attempt by the AI.
Under this assumption, we will define a measure of the dispersion of AI-exposed steps in a workflow, which we refer to as the \emph{fragmentation index} of a workflow.
We then show that the fragmentation index of a workflow approximates (up to constant factors) the cost of an optimal AI strategy for that workflow.
In other words, workflows for which the fragmentation index is high will tend to yield less benefit from AI automation.
Notably, this measure speaks to the chaining problem and abstracts from job design, which we take up in Section~\ref{sec:longrun}.

Intuitively, the fragmentation index is motivated by a hypothetical scenario where a prophet can ``see the future'' to determine which tasks would be completed successfully by AI on its first attempt and which would not.
If many tasks in sequence will all complete successfully on the first try, these are a good candidate for an AI chain.
The prophet might therefore chain together all contiguous sequences of tasks that would all succeed, and perform all other tasks manually.
The fragmentation index is then the expected cost of this strategy, under the assumption that the prophet's foresight is correct.
Notably, this AI strategy isn't necessarily optimal even with the benefit of foresight: a long AI chain with a good probability of success might be optimal even if the prophet knows that it will fail on the first attempt.
However, what we show is that its cost approximates the cost of the optimal strategy even without foresight.

To define the fragmentation index more formally, suppose that each step $s_i$ succeeds independently with probability $q_i$; any task that does not succeed is said to fail.
Write $F$ for the set of steps that fail, and $\mathcal{C} = \{C_1, \dotsc, C_k\}$ for the random variable representing the collection of maximal connected components of non-failed steps.
The weight of each $C_j \in \mathcal{C}$ is defined to be $\omega(C_j) = \min\{ 1, \sum_{i \in C_j} \manualTime{i} \}$.
That is, each $C_j$ has weight $1$ unless the sum of the manual costs of its steps is less than $1$ (due to our assumption that AI management costs are normalized to $1$).

Given a realization of $\mathcal{C}$ and $F$, we define the realized fragmentation to be
\begin{equation}
    \sum_{i \in F}\min\left\{\manualTime{i}, \frac{\AItime{i}}{q_i}\right\} + \sum_{C_j \in \mathcal{C}} \omega(C_j).\label{eq:fragmentation}
\end{equation}
The \emph{fragmentation index} is defined to be the expected value of the realized fragmentation.

Intuitively, we expect the fragmentation index to be lower when highly automatable steps (that is, those for which the AI are likely to succeed) are clustered together, since a large cluster of highly automatable steps are more likely to realize as a single connected component when failures are realized.

We will show that the fragmentation index is within a constant factor of the cost of the optimal AI strategy for a given step sequence.

\begin{proposition}
\label{prop:fragmentation}
   Fix a sequence of $m$ steps $\mathcal{S} = \{s_1, \dotsc, s_m\}$, each with normalized AI management cost $\AItime{i} = 1$.
   Let $FI$ denote its fragmentation index and let $OPT$ denote the minimum cost over all AI strategies.
   Then $\tfrac{1}{8}OPT \leq FI \leq \tfrac{5}{4}OPT$.
   If we further assume $\manualTime{i} \geq 1$ for all $i$, then $\tfrac{1}{4}OPT \leq FI \leq \tfrac{5}{4}OPT$.
\end{proposition}

The key take-away from Proposition~\ref{prop:fragmentation} is that workflows with high fragmentation index (i.e., those for which the expected number of \emph{consecutive} successful step executions by an AI is low) will tend to have higher costs even under optimal use of AI chaining.
A key driver of efficiency gains via AI automation is therefore not simply the expected number of steps that can be automated effectively, but the number of \emph{adjacent} steps in the workflow that have high exposure to AI.

An implication of our proof of Proposition~\ref{prop:fragmentation} is a natural and approximately optimal greedy algorithm for constructing AI strategies.
The algorithm groups tasks into chains as long as the probability of success is sufficiently high; if the success probability falls too low then the chain is terminated and a new chain is started (with chains of length 1 converted to manual execution when appropriate).



\subsection{Non-Monotonicity of Gains from AI Improvements}
\label{sec:nonlinear}

Improvements to AI technology naturally lead to improvements in the overall cost of production, but these effects need not be monotone.
The full economic impact of a disruptive general-purpose technology can take significant time and investment to materialize, with notable historical examples ranging from the steam engine to electricity to computers.
In each case, a new technology enables modest short-term improvements to existing workflows, but the full impact is not felt until large-scale reorganization of production is enabled \citep{brynjolfsson2021productivity}.
This drives a potentially non-monotone marginal value from technology improvements, where major improvements are only unlocked after surpassing a threshold of capability at which they become feasible.

Our framework captures such non-monotonicities in the marginal value of technological improvements.
This arises in our model via optimization over different AI deployment strategies.
One can think of parameter $\alpha \in (0,1]$ in our model as a measure of the quality of a general purpose AI technology, taken to be the probability that a task of normalized difficulty $1$ is completed successfully.
We can then consider general technology improvements as increasing the value of $\alpha$.
When AI quality is so low that the firm deploys AI nowhere, marginal improvements in $\alpha$ offer no return at all: every step is performed manually, and production costs do not depend on AI quality.
Once $\alpha$ passes the level at which deploying AI somewhere in the workflow becomes worthwhile, further improvements pay off, but at a diminishing rate, as a more reliable AI is being applied to a fixed set of AI-executed steps.
And when $\alpha$ reaches a level at which the firm reorganizes its deployment strategy, extending a chain to automate steps that were previously manual, the marginal return to AI quality jumps up discontinuously, because quality improvements now compound over a longer stretch of the workflow.
We refer to the values of $\alpha$ at which the optimal strategy changes as \emph{reorganization thresholds}: they partition the range of AI quality into regimes, within which the returns to AI progress diminish, while at each threshold the returns jump upward.
The example below shows this pattern at work in a two-step workflow.

\begin{example}
\label{ex:nonmonotone}

\[ \bigl(\manualTime{i},\, \AItime{i},\, d_i\bigr)_{i=1,2} = (6,\,4,\,12),\quad (8,\,4,\,1). \]
At an AI quality of about $\alpha = 0.9$, these difficulties deliver the success probabilities used in those examples, $q_1 = 0.28$ and $q_2 = 0.9$.

The non-monotonicity in marginal returns to improving AI is a general phenomenon, and we formalize it in Appendix~\ref{app:nonmonotone}.
There we show that the cost of any fixed AI strategy varies smoothly with $\alpha$, so that the firm's optimal cost is the lower envelope of finitely many smooth cost curves, and prove in Lemma~\ref{lem:reorg_threshold} that the marginal gains from improving AI diminish monotonically within each regime where a single strategy remains optimal and jump upward at every reorganization threshold.

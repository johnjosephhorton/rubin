This section briefly provides two additional results that the framework delivers, but defers their detailed discussion to the appendix.
Section~\ref{sec:extensions.aggregation} reconciles our firm-level, sequential model of production where tasks and jobs are determined endogenously with models that study the implications of automation from a macro perspective by aggregating many firms' production function into a single CES production function at the macro level.
Section~\ref{sec:extensions.computation} provides algorithms showing how the cost minimization problems we have posed for firm's short- and long-run optimization can actually be solved in polynomial time.


\subsection{CES Representation}
\label{sec:extensions.aggregation}

So far we have focused on a single firm's production: which steps it chains together, where it draws the boundaries of jobs, and what those jobs pay workers.
What ultimately matters, however, is how these firm-level adjustments translate into the broader labor market: how much labor the economy employs, how output responds to capital and to labor, and what becomes of both as AI improves.
These are the terms the automation literature works in, writing production functions directly at the level of the aggregate economy \citep{acemoglu2018, acemoglu2022artificial, acemoglu2024tasks}.

Our framework can be expressed in the same terms.
We distinguish two types of labor: \emph{manual labor}, which performs steps directly, and \emph{AI management labor}, which prompts the AI and verifies what it returns.\footnote{
The distinction between these two types of labor is deliberate rather than necessary.
One could carry the aggregation through with a single type of labor, pooling the two requirements into one input.
We keep them apart because the model already distinguishes the skill a step demands when a worker performs it, $\manualSkill{i}$, from the skill it demands when a worker manages an AI attempt at it, $\AIskill{i}$, so two types of labor is the natural counterpart of a distinction we have already drawn.
}
Appendix~\ref{sec:aggregation} shows that the firm-level technology developed earlier, in which the content of tasks and of jobs are chosen rather than given, then aggregates to
\begin{align}
\label{eq:lr_macro_ces}
X = \left(\theta_{\AIletter}\,\aggregateAIlabor^{\rho} + \theta_{\manualLetter}\,\aggregateManualLabor^{\rho} + (1-\theta_{\AIletter}-\theta_{\manualLetter})\,K^{\rho}\right)^{\frac{1}{\rho}},
\end{align}
over economy-wide AI management labor $\aggregateAIlabor$, manual labor $\aggregateManualLabor$, and capital $K$, with $X$ denoting aggregate output.\footnote{
The representation asks more of the economy than the firm-level argument does: capital productivity is common across firms, every firm organizes identically, and realized effective AI quality is the single dimension along which firms differ.
}
This is the familiar constant elasticity of substitution (CES) form, with which the macroeconomic implications of automation are usually studied.

Arriving at \eqref{eq:lr_macro_ces} is not straightforward, and it is worth stating what makes it work.
No individual firm in our model substitutes AI management labor for manual labor, since producing a unit of output requires completing production steps in fixed proportions, so each firm's technology is Leontief.
Aggregate substitution comes instead from composition, in the manner of \citet{houthakker1955pareto} and \citet{levhari1968note}: firms have access to the same AI technology but differ in how effectively they are able to deploy it, and when AI management labor becomes relatively cheaper the economy's input mix moves because the set of firms for which using AI is worthwhile has moved, not because any firm has changed its own mix.
Appendix~\ref{sec:aggregation} obtains that dispersion from the order in which a firm has to commit, settling on its AI strategy and job design before it learns how effectively it can put AI to use, and derives in closed form the distribution of realized AI effectiveness under which \eqref{eq:lr_macro_ces} holds.

The micro and macro representations of production are therefore not in competition.
Our account of how chaining redraws tasks and jobs is not an alternative to the aggregate view of automation but a foundation for it, and the implications people draw from improving AI quality in a CES economy carry over intact.
The organizational content carries over as well, but only in the parameters. 
That is, two economies whose production steps are identically exposed to AI, differing only in how those steps are arranged within workflows, reach the same functional form expressed by \eqref{eq:lr_macro_ces} but with different weights and input requirements while looking alike on any step-level exposure measure.
This is why workflow-level objects such as the fragmentation index have to be measured where the arrangement of work is still visible, which is what our empirical evaluation in Section~\ref{sec:empirics} does.


\subsection{Computing the Firm's Optimum}
\label{sec:extensions.computation}

Throughout the paper we have assumed the firm can obtain its cost-minimizing arrangement without discussing how that arrangement is found.
Chaining is what makes this a substantive question.
A step absorbed into a chain costs nothing directly, but it adds its failure risk to every attempt at the chain, so whether automating it pays depends on the arrangement the step sits in rather than on the step alone.
The firm cannot solve its optimization problem step by step, then, and must instead compare all feasible arrangements. 
A production containing $m$ steps admits exponentially many arrangements.

That exponential count overstates the difficulty, however, because the arrangements overlap heavily.
Any two that treat the first stretch of the workflow alike cost the same over that stretch, so the firm need not calculate it twice.
Both the short- and long-run problems can therefore be built up from the front, one step at a time, with the work spent on an early stretch reused for every arrangement of what follows.
The two optimization problems differ only in how much a settled stretch has to remember about itself.
In the short run its time cost is all that matters, and the optimum is reached exactly.
In the long run it must also carry the skill and the time of the worker whose job is still not terminated, and exactness gives way to an approximation the firm can make as tight as it likes.
We take the two horizons in turn and defer the proofs to the appendix.

\paragraph{The Short Run Optimization.}
With the job and its wage fixed, the firm minimizes total time \eqref{eq:totalcost_shortterm}.
For $k \leq m$, let $C[k]$ denote the minimum time needed to complete a hypothetical workflow containing only steps $1$ through $k$, so that $C[0] = 0$ and $C[m]$ is the optimum.
Step $k$ is either performed manually, or augmented as the endpoint of an AI chain reaching back to some earlier step $\ell$; in either case the steps before that point are optimized separately, which gives
\begin{align}
\label{eq:shortrun_recursion}
C[k] = \min\Biggl\{ \underbrace{C[k-1] + \manualTime{k}}_{\text{step $k$ manual}}, \ \underbrace{\min_{\ell < k}\ \Bigl( C[\ell] + \frac{\AItime{k}}{\prod_{i=\ell+1}^{k} q_i} \Bigr)}_{\text{step $k$ ends a chain begun at } \ell+1} \Biggr\}.
\end{align}
Because time costs enter \eqref{eq:totalcost_shortterm} additively, a stretch's time cost is all that \eqref{eq:shortrun_recursion} needs from it, and the position $k$ indexes the table on its own.
Each of the $m$ values costs $O(m)$ to evaluate, since the chain ending at step $k$ may begin anywhere before it.

\begin{proposition}[Short-run AI Deployment]
\label{prop:shortrun_dp}
Given a workflow with $m$ steps, the AI strategy minimizing \eqref{eq:totalcost_shortterm} can be calculated in time $O(m^2)$ via dynamic programming.
\end{proposition}

\paragraph{The Long Run Optimization.}
The firm now chooses job boundaries as well, and \eqref{eq:totalcost_with_handoff} multiplies each job's total skill by its total time.
A task's contribution to cost therefore depends on how much skill and how much time its job already carries, so the position alone no longer summarizes a stretch and we carry two further coordinates.
Let $W(i,\, \skillcost{},\, \timecost{})$ be the minimum cost of completing steps $1$ through $i$ when the worker currently accumulating tasks, the \emph{active worker}, already holds total skill $\skillcost{}$ and total time $\timecost{}$, the latter including their hand-off, and let $V(i) = W(i,\, 0,\, \handofftime{i})$ be the corresponding cost when that worker's job closes at step $i$.
The base case is $W(0,\, \skillcost{},\, \timecost{}) = \skillcost{}\,\timecost{}$, and the firm faces three options at each step rather than two,
\begin{align}
\label{eq:longrun_recursion}
W(i,\, \skillcost{},\, \timecost{}) = \min\Biggl\{ \underbrace{\skillcost{}\,\timecost{} + V(i)}_{\text{close the job}}, \ \underbrace{W\bigl(i-1,\, \skillcost{} + \manualSkill{i},\, \timecost{} + \manualTime{i}\bigr)}_{\text{step $i$ manual}}, \ \underbrace{\min_{r < i}\ W\Bigl(r,\, \skillcost{} + \AIskill{i},\, \timecost{} + \tfrac{\AItime{i}}{\prod_{s=r+1}^{i} q_s}\Bigr)}_{\text{step $i$ ends a chain begun at } r+1} \Biggr\},
\end{align}
with the optimum at $V(m) = W(m,\, 0,\, 0)$.
Closing a job is where the accumulated skill and time are finally multiplied into a wage bill, which is why the state has to carry both.
Both are continuous, so the table cannot be filled exactly, and we round each coordinate to powers of $(1+\epsilon)$.
Skill then lies in $[1/B,\, mB]$ and time in $[1/B,\, 2mB^3]$, each range holding $O(\epsilon^{-1}\log(mB))$ such powers, so the table has $O(m\,\epsilon^{-2}\log^2(mB))$ entries and $O(m)$ work per entry.

\begin{proposition}[Long-run AI Deployment and Job Design]
\label{prop:totalcost_optimization_dp}
Fix a workflow with $m$ steps whose skill, time, and hand-off costs lie in $[1/B, B]$ for some $B > 0$.
For any $\epsilon > 0$, a pair of AI strategy $\T$ and job design $\J$ whose long-run cost~\eqref{eq:totalcost_with_handoff} is within a factor $(1+\epsilon)$ of the minimum can be computed in time $O(m^2 \epsilon^{-2} \log^2(mB))$ via dynamic programming.
\end{proposition}

Appendices~\ref{app:shortrun_proof} and~\ref{app:jobdesign.longrun} give the proofs, including the bound on the error the rounding introduces.

Together these results also suggest a use for the framework beyond what we do here.
Paired with detailed measurement of the time and skill content of individual tasks, the algorithms above could be run on real workflows to ask how a given advance in AI capability would redraw them.
